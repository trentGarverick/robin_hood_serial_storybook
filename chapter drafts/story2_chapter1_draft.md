# Story 2: The Weaver's Debt
## Part One: A Matter of Roofs

*(Working titles — happy to change either.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — Tom Weaver's small home/workshop, a loom visible, Hob nearby, a sense of quiet dread]**

Tom Weaver had built his loom with his own hands, eleven years ago, in the same corner of the same room his son had been born in two years later — a fact he mentioned rather more often than strictly necessary, in the manner of a man who found comfort in reciting the things that were still, for the moment, true.

It was, this particular morning, considerably less true than usual. A folded notice sat on the table where breakfast should have been, and had sat there long enough that nobody in the house had worked up the courage to move it, as though ignoring a piece of parchment might eventually convince it to reconsider what it said.

"Da," said Hob, not for the first time, "what does it *say*."

Tom Weaver did not look up from the loom he wasn't actually working. "It says the roof over your head has a price on it now. That's the whole of it, stripped down."

"There's a new tax," he said eventually, because Hob was nine and nine-year-olds could generally tell when they were being managed rather than answered, and deserved better than that. "On dwellings. Sudden. Steep. Man came through a fortnight past collecting it on behalf of somebody called Vane, who's apparently bought himself the *right* to collect taxes in this district, same as another man might buy a herd of sheep." He said the name the way some men said a curse they were trying not to say in front of a child. "We haven't got it. Not by half. Not by considerably more than half."

"What happens if we don't pay?"

Tom Weaver did not answer that one directly, which told Hob most of what he needed to know regardless.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Hob running through the village to find Wat, urgency in his posture]**

Hob found Wat Hale exactly where Wat Hale generally was, which was somewhere he technically wasn't supposed to be, doing something he technically hadn't been given permission to do — in this instance, attempting to teach a chicken to walk in a straight line, with limited success.

"I need you to introduce me to your outlaws," Hob said, without preamble, arriving at a dead run and considerably out of breath.

Wat considered this with the gravity the request deserved. "They're not really *my* outlaws."

"You know them better than I do."

"That's true," Wat allowed. "What's happened?"

Hob told him. It did not take long. It rarely does, when the thing that's happened is simple and unfair in roughly equal measure.

Wat was quiet for exactly as long as it took him to finish considering the problem, which was not very long at all, because nine-year-olds who have already watched their own family nearly lose everything once tend to arrive at conclusions faster than most.

"Right," he said. "Come on, then. They're camped past the old mill road, or they were last I heard, and if they've moved it won't have been far — Marian doesn't like moving further than she has to, on account of the arrows."

"Will they even listen? To us?"

"They listened to me," Wat said, with the simple confidence of a boy who had, in fact, been listened to exactly once by exactly this group of people, and had accordingly filed the experience under *things that are generally true.*

---

**[PLATE 3 — the two boys arriving at Sherwood Camp, the crew looking up, a sense of the group being approached with a real problem]**

They found the camp without much trouble, which said something about how thoroughly its residents had stopped worrying about being found by anyone dangerous, and rather a lot about how little effort they'd put into hiding from two determined nine-year-olds.

"There's a boy here," Long John announced, unnecessarily, since everyone could see there were, in point of fact, two.

"Wat," said Marian, looking up from an arrow she was fletching, "and a friend. What's happened."

It was not, notably, phrased as a question, because Marian had long since learned that a boy arriving out of breath rarely had anything but bad news to deliver, and had adjusted her greetings accordingly.

Hob told it again, more carefully this time, with Wat filling in the parts he rushed past and Robin listening with the particular stillness of a man recognizing, uncomfortably, a shape he already knew. A family. A debt manufactured faster than it could reasonably be paid. A name — Vane — attached to a scheme that wore the Sheriff's authority like a well-tailored coat while somehow managing to look even less deserved on him.

"How much," Marian asked, when Hob had finished, "and by when."

Hob told her that too. The number, spoken aloud, had the particular weight of a sum that sounds almost reasonable until you consider who's actually meant to produce it.

"That's not impossible," Marian said, slowly, in the tone of a woman already doing arithmetic she hadn't been asked to do. "It's just very close to it."

---

**[PLATE 4 — the group's reaction, Robin's resolve visible, the others exchanging looks — the decision beginning to form]**

"We're helping," Robin said. It was not, strictly, his decision alone to make, and he seemed to realize this roughly half a second after saying it, glancing around at the others with the particular expression of a man hoping his conviction would retroactively count as consensus.

"Obviously we're helping," said Will, checking his sleeve less out of vanity this time than simple habit. "The question is how, given that none of us are, as a rule, in possession of that kind of coin."

"We don't need to *have* it," said Marian. "We need to take it from somewhere it's already sitting, unearned, in a man's strongbox." She looked at Hob and Wat with something that might, on anyone less careful with her expressions, have been called a smile. "Which is, conveniently, rather the whole reason we exist."

"Vane," said Brother Crumb, thoughtfully, from where he was stirring something that smelled considerably better than the conversation warranted. "I know that name. Buys up tax rights in districts nobody wealthy wants to be bothered administering directly, skims his own margin on top of whatever the Sheriff's already taking, and does it all from a manor house he's very proud of, three days' ride from here." He tasted whatever was in the pot, found it acceptable, and continued. "A man who buys the right to squeeze the poor generally keeps rather a lot of squeezed coin sitting close to hand. Habit of the trade."

"You know a great deal about tax farmers, for a friar," Long John observed.

"I know a great deal about larders," said Crumb. "The principle's the same. Follow where the excess collects, and you'll generally find the man who collected it."

---

**[PLATE 5 — closing: the group gathered, a rough plan beginning to take shape, Hob and Wat included in the circle, forward momentum]**

"Three days' ride," Robin repeated. "How long have we got?"

"A fortnight, near enough," said Hob. "Da said the collector's due back to confirm payment or take the house, whichever's ready first."

"Then we've time to do this properly," said Marian, "which is more than we usually have, and I intend to make the most of it." She looked around at the others — Robin's determination, Will already turning the shape of a plan over with the particular relish of a man who'd found a target worth his talents, Long John quietly working out the practical logistics of getting five people and their nonsense three days closer to a stranger's manor house without anyone noticing.

"Well," said Brother Crumb, into the settling quiet, ladling out a bowl for each of the two boys without being asked. "Eat first. Planning goes better on a full stomach, in my experience, and considerably worse on an empty one."

Hob, holding a bowl of something that smelled far better than anything in his own kitchen had in weeks, looked at Wat with an expression somewhere between relief and disbelief.

"Told you," said Wat, with the deep, uncomplicated satisfaction of a boy who had, for once, been entirely right about something.
