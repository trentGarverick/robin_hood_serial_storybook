# Story 4: The Company She Keeps
## Part One: What Pip Carried

*(Working titles — happy to change either. This is the reveal chapter — please read carefully before I build it, since it locks in major permanent facts.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — Pip leading Marian away from camp, into a quiet clearing, the firelight receding behind them]**

Pip led her further than Marian had expected — past the treeline, past the sound of the camp settling for the night, into a small clearing she hadn't known was there despite months of considering these woods, in some fundamental sense, home. Moonlight found its way through the canopy in patches. Somewhere behind them, faint and getting fainter, Will's voice carried on about something that had stopped mattering the moment they'd left.

Pip stopped, finally, and turned to face her properly, and there was something in the set of his shoulders — squared, deliberate, like a person bracing for a task rather than beginning a conversation — that told Marian, before a single word had been said, that whatever came next was not going to be small.

"I owe you a great deal of honesty," Pip said. "I've been putting it off for longer than I should have."

"You don't owe me anything," Marian said, careful, not yet certain which direction this was going to fall, though certain enough, by now, that it was going to fall *somewhere.*

"I do, actually." Pip's voice had changed — not dramatically, but perceptibly, some careful boyish cadence dropping away like a coat set down at the end of a long day. "You've been kind to me. Kinder than you knew you had reason to be, since you didn't have the whole of it. I'd like to give you the whole of it now, if you'll let me."

"I'm listening," Marian said, and meant it entirely.

Pip took a breath — a real one, the kind that precedes something that has been held a long time — and began.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Pip revealing herself: removing the cap, a visible shift in posture and presentation, the moment of true unveiling]**

"My name is Pip," she said. "That part was always true. The rest of it — the boy, the grandson, all of it — that part wasn't. I'm not a boy, Marian. I never have been."

She removed the cap she'd worn every day Marian had known her, and something in her posture shifted with it — not dramatically, not a costume falling away so much as a held breath finally released, a young woman standing where a boy had stood a moment before, the same face, the same watchful eyes, simply no longer performing anything.

"I'm nineteen," Pip said. "I know I read younger. That's rather the point, most days."

Marian, who had suspected — who had known, on some level, for months now, in the specific unspoken way she knew most things before she let herself say them aloud — found that the confirmation still landed with real weight.

"Alright," she said, quietly. "Go on."

---

*(new text page)*

"I dressed as a boy first out of necessity," Pip said, sitting now on a fallen log, the moonlight catching her properly for what felt, to both of them, like the first time. "There was a marriage arranged for me — I won't bore you with whose, or why, except to say it wasn't a kindness, and the man in question had buried two wives already in ways nobody official ever asked too many questions about. I was fourteen. I ran, dressed as I could to travel safest, and I never quite stopped."

"That's not boring," Marian said. "Don't call it that."

Pip's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It's easier, some days, to make it small. I've had a long time to practice."

---

**[PLATE 3 — Pip continuing her story: how Thistlewood found her, the choice to keep the disguise, the practical and personal reasons intertwined]**

"Thistlewood found me on the road, half-starved, still dressed as a boy because I hadn't worked out yet how to be anything else safely. He didn't ask me to change it. Told me a granddaughter traveling with an old man draws one kind of attention, and a grandson draws a considerably safer kind, and let me decide for myself which I preferred." She looked down at the cap still in her hands, turning it over. "I kept choosing the boy. Some of it was safety — genuinely was, still is, most places we go. Some of it, if I'm honest, was that I liked having somewhere to hide that wasn't just running anymore. It's harder to be afraid of a life you've built a name inside of."

"And now?" Marian asked, gently.

"Now I'm tired of hiding from the one person who's already worked out there's something to hide," Pip said. "You've earned better than my caution, Marian. You have for a while."

---

*(new text page)*

Marian sat with that for a long moment, turning it over with the same careful attention she gave everything that actually mattered, and found, when she finally spoke, that the question waiting underneath all her collected suspicion came out simpler than she'd expected it to.

"And Thistlewood," she said. "The staff. The horse that stumbled at exactly the right moment. The things that keep happening around this camp that no one but you and I ever seem to properly question." She held Pip's gaze, steady, done pretending the pieces didn't fit together the way they plainly did. "He's not merely a clever old man, is he."

Pip was quiet for a moment — not evasive, simply choosing the words with real care.

"No," she said. "He isn't."

---

**[PLATE 4 — Pip confirming the truth about Thistlewood: the debt, the wizard, the full picture at last]**

"He's a wizard," Pip said, plainly, the word landing in the clearing with none of the drama such a word ought, by rights, to carry, simply the weight of a fact finally spoken aloud. "A genuine one. Older than he looks, though he looks old enough as it is. He owes a debt — a gambling debt, of all things, which I've always found rather undignified for a man of his talents — to someone who called it in on very particular terms."

"What terms?"

"Keep Robin safe," Pip said. "Alive. Out of the noose. That's the whole of it, and it's why he's been doing what he's been doing since before any of you knew he existed — the luck, the accidents, the things that always seem to break your way at precisely the moment they need to. None of it's luck. It never has been."

---

*(new text page)*

Marian absorbed this the way she absorbed most difficult truths — quietly, methodically, already reorganizing everything she thought she'd understood into a shape that finally, properly fit.

"Who called in the debt," she asked, already half-certain of the answer before she'd finished asking it.

"Robin's father," said Pip. "He's never stopped watching out for his son. He simply couldn't do it directly, so he found another way."

Marian closed her eyes for a moment, the weight of that — the sheer, aching irony of it, a father Robin believed had abandoned him having spent this entire time doing exactly the opposite in the only language available to him — settling into her chest with an ache she hadn't expected.

"Robin can't know," she said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Pip agreed. "Not yet. Perhaps not for a long while yet. That part isn't mine to give you permission to change, and it isn't yours either, however much either of us might wish otherwise."

---

**[PLATE 5 — the quiet aftermath: Marian and Pip sitting together in the clearing, a new understanding settled between them]**

They sat together a long while after that, the conversation settling into something quieter, less urgent, two people who had each been carrying something heavy discovering the particular relief of finally setting part of it down in the same place.

"Why tonight," Marian asked, eventually. "Why now, of all the evenings you might have chosen."

"The Sheriff," Pip admitted. "Whatever he's planning, it's going to be worse than anything that's come before it, and I couldn't bear the thought of you walking into it only half-informed, still trying to work out on your own whether you could trust what your own eyes kept telling you. You deserved better than that. You've *earned* better than that, a hundred times over, and I was tired of being the reason you didn't have it."

"Thank you," Marian said, simply, and found she meant it more completely than the small words seemed capable of carrying.

---

*(new text page)*

"You won't tell the others," Pip said — not quite a question, though it carried enough uncertainty underneath the words that Marian understood exactly how much trust was being extended alongside the asking.

"No," Marian said. "Not Robin. Not any of them. Not until you or Thistlewood decide it's time, or until keeping it costs more than it protects." She held Pip's gaze, steady, the particular unshakeable steadiness that had, by now, become the thing the others relied on most about her without ever quite naming it. "I'm rather good at carrying things quietly. You may have noticed."

"I had," Pip said, and this time the almost-smile made it all the way to something real. "It's rather why I chose you."

They walked back to camp separately, a few minutes apart, the way people do when they've shared something too large to carry back into ordinary company all at once — and if Marian's face, by the time she reached the firelight, had settled back into its usual careful composure, the woman underneath it had changed in ways that would take considerably longer than one walk through the dark to fully understand.
