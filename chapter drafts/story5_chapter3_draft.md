# Story 5: The Sheriff's Reckoning
## Part Three: A Colder Kind of Hunting

*(Working title — happy to change.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — camp life, Robin's archery mishap, comedic opening]**

Robin had taken, in the tense days since the ridge, to practicing considerably more than usual, on the theory — never quite stated aloud, but obvious to everyone watching — that an archer who couldn't hit anything on purpose ought at least to look like he was trying.

He drew. He loosed. The arrow sailed a full four feet wide of the target and buried itself instead in the side of Brother Crumb's second-best cooking pot, which responded to the intrusion with a long, mournful *clang* and a thin ribbon of stew beginning, with great dignity, to leak out onto the ground.

"That was my *lunch*," said Brother Crumb, with the particular betrayal of a man watching a genuinely good stew die slowly in front of him.

"I'll get you another pot," Robin offered.

"You'll get me another *lunch*," Crumb corrected, "which is a considerably more urgent problem."

*"He's getting worse,"* Barley observed, watching the stew's slow progress across the dirt with real interest.

*"He's getting nervous,"* Gideon corrected. *"There's a difference. Nervous men aim worse than usual men, and worse men aim worse than Robin usually does, which puts him somewhere near the bottom of a fairly short list."*

*"That's almost sympathetic, coming from you,"* said Grimalkin.

*"I contain multitudes,"* said Gideon, which everyone present — animal and human alike — had by now learned to accept as the entire explanation he intended to offer for anything.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Abbot Percival arriving with news, warm comedic beat with Brother Crumb before serious tone lands]**

It was Abbot Percival, of all people, who arrived not an hour later, robes dusty from travel and expression set in its usual weary disapproval — though today, notably, the disapproval seemed aimed at something considerably larger than his wayward kitchener.

"Crumb," he said, by way of greeting, surveying the ruined stew with visible sympathy despite himself. "I see standards remain as low as ever."

"Lower, if anything," Crumb agreed, entirely without offense. "You didn't walk all this way to insult my cooking, Abbot. What's happened?"

"No," Percival admitted, and something in his voice made even Will, still checking his sleeve in the background, go quiet and attentive. "I came because there's a man asking questions in the villages, and I thought you'd rather hear it from me than from someone with less reason to be careful about the telling."

*(new text page)*

"What sort of questions?" Marian asked, already alert.

"About witches," Percival said, with the particular discomfort of a religious man forced to use a word he considered mostly nonsense in a professional context. "Church-sent, this one — a Witchfinder, they're calling him, investigating reports out of the district near where Vane's little... incident... occurred. Talk of a goat that felled grown men. A staff that moved faster than any old man's arm should allow. The sort of story that grows more elaborate with every retelling, and this man seems entirely dedicated to collecting every version of it."

"That's absurd," Will said, though the laugh he'd clearly intended came out considerably thinner than planned.

"Is it," said Percival, mild, "given present company," and let the silence that followed do the rest of the work for him.

---

**[PLATE 3 — the crew scouting a village, observing Witchfinder Crane interviewing a villager, comedic exaggeration mixed with real unease]**

They found him three days later, in a village close enough to the ambush site that the story had clearly had ample time to grow roots — a spare, unremarkable man in plain dark clothes, seated with patient stillness while an elderly villager, warming visibly to his audience, delivered an account that bore only the loosest possible relationship to anything that had actually happened.

"—and the goat," the old man was saying, with the relish of a man who had told this story a dozen times and improved it every occasion, "*looked* at him. Just looked. And the fellow went down like he'd been struck by lightning, sir, struck clean down, and the goat's eyes — I'll swear this on anything you like — the goat's eyes were *burning*."

"They were not burning," Gideon muttered, deeply offended, to no ears but the animals' own. *"I headbutted him. There was no eye involvement whatsoever."*

---

*(new text page)*

*"He's rather enjoying the burning-eyes part, though,"* Barley observed.

*"I am absolutely not enjoying it,"* said Gideon, in the tone of an animal who was, in fact, enjoying it a little.

Robin, crouched nearby and doing his level best not to laugh loud enough to give their position away, caught Marian's eye and found her fighting the exact same battle, which somehow made the whole thing considerably harder to keep quiet about.

"A goat with burning eyes," Will whispered, delighted despite himself. "I take back what I said. This might be the single greatest thing that's ever happened to us."

"It will stop being funny," Marian whispered back, not unkindly, "the moment he separates the nonsense from the parts that are actually true. Watch him. Not the old man — *him*."

---

**[PLATE 4 — Witchfinder Crane himself: cold, methodical, unsettling, the comedic tone dropping away]**

And watching him, properly, the humor did drain out of the moment rather quickly. Crane took notes throughout with a small, careful hand, asking follow-up questions in a flat, unhurried voice that gave away nothing of what he actually believed — not dismissing the burning eyes outright, but not accepting them either, sorting instead through the old man's account with the patient, methodical precision of someone separating wheat from chaff one grain at a time.

"And the staff," Crane said, when the old man finally paused for breath. "You mentioned an old man with a staff, earlier. Tell me about him again. Slowly, this time. I want every detail you can recall, however small it seems to you."

The old man, delighted to have a second audience for his favorite part, began again from the beginning.

---

*(new text page)*

"He's not interested in the goat," Marian breathed, watching Crane's careful, unhurried attention. "Not really. The goat's a curiosity. The staff — the old man — that's what he actually came for."

"He doesn't believe in witchcraft," Robin said, slowly, working it through. "Does he."

"I don't think it matters whether he believes in it," Marian said. "I think he believes there's *something* real underneath all the exaggeration, and he intends to find it regardless of what name he ends up putting on it." She watched Crane close his small notebook with quiet, unhurried finality, the humor of the burning-eyed goat entirely gone from her now. "That's considerably more dangerous than a man who simply believes in magic. A man like that can be dismissed. A man looking for the truth underneath the story cannot."

---

**[PLATE 5 — closing: the crew withdrawing, tension restored, real dread underneath the earlier comedy]**

They withdrew well before Crane finished his interviews, unwilling to risk a second near-discovery so soon after the last one, and didn't speak properly again until the village was well behind them and the forest had closed back in around them like something protective, or at least something that wanted to be.

"So," said Will, eventually, the earlier delight gone entirely from his voice. "Vane wants us caught. Blackwood wants us found. And now there's a third one who wants to know *what* we actually are, which is rather worse than either of the other two, when you say it plainly like that."

"When you say it plainly," Marian agreed, "yes." She looked back once, toward the village and the man in it, patient and methodical and entirely unhurried, and found, for the first time since any of this had started, that the weight of what they were actually protecting felt heavier than it had in a very long while. "We'll need to be considerably more careful than we have been. About all of it. Starting now."
