# Story 3: The Devil's Own Luck
## Part Two: Word from Ashcombe

*(Working title — happy to change.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — camp life settling into its new normal, animals lounging around, Thistlewood and Pip visiting from the cottage]**

Life at Sherwood Camp had acquired, in the weeks since Vane's retreat, a rhythm none of them would have predicted and all of them had quietly come to enjoy — Thistlewood and Pip wandering over from the new cottage often enough that their visits had stopped being occasions and started being simply Tuesdays, and the three animals treating the whole arrangement as though it had always been theirs by right.

Gideon had claimed a particular patch of sun near the fire pit and defended it with the quiet, unshakeable confidence of a creature who had never once lost an argument about territory. Grimalkin occupied Marian's lap more often than not these days, a fact she had stopped even pretending to find remarkable. Barley simply orbited whoever looked most likely to produce food, which was, on any given afternoon, an excellent description of Brother Crumb.

*"He's going to make himself sick,"* Grimalkin observed, watching Barley attempt to accept a third helping of something from Crumb with an enthusiasm that outpaced his actual capacity.

*"He says that every time,"* said Gideon, not opening his eyes.

*"I'm right every time."*

*"You're right,"* Gideon agreed, *"and it changes nothing, which is rather the tragedy of being right about Barley specifically."*

From across the camp, entirely unaware of being discussed, Barley accepted the third helping with the delighted commitment of a creature who had never once regretted a decision in his life, and would not, evidently, be starting today.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Wat and Hob arriving with Ellen, a woman from Ashcombe, urgency in their approach]**

It was Wat, predictably, who spotted them first — two boys and a woman none of the camp recognized, moving with the particular hurried purpose that had become, by now, a fairly reliable sign of trouble on its way.

"This is Ellen," Wat announced, arriving slightly ahead of the others. "She weaves. She knew Tom, before — well, before everything."

Ellen looked to be somewhere in her middle years, work-roughened hands, an expression that carried its exhaustion carefully, the way people do when they've had considerable practice not collapsing under it in public. "Tom sent word what you did for him," she said, without preamble. "Said if anyone might help Ashcombe, it'd be worth the walk to ask."

"Ashcombe," Robin repeated. "What's happened?"

---

*(new text page)*

"Taxes," Ellen said, and the word came out of her with the particular flatness of someone who had said it so many times it had stopped meaning anything except *the thing that is ruining us.* "New ones. Steep ones. The Sheriff's deputies came through a month back and haven't stopped coming back since — every family in Ashcombe's given up something they couldn't spare, and there's precious little left to give that isn't a roof or a child's next meal."

"That's the Sheriff himself, then," Marian said, carefully. "Not another Vane."

"His own men, this time," Ellen confirmed. "No middleman to blame it on. Just the Sheriff's collectors, and a great deal of coin leaving Ashcombe that isn't coming back."

Brother Crumb, who had gone very still somewhere in the middle of Ellen's account, set down what he'd been cooking with the particular care of a man setting aside something he intended to return to properly furious.

---

**[PLATE 3 — the planning discussion beginning, the whole gang gathered, Thistlewood and Pip present but peripheral, animals nearby]**

"There's more," Ellen said, once the initial anger had settled enough to make room for the rest of it. "My brother works the stables at the collection house. He says the coin's not staying in Ashcombe — it's being moved. Through Sherwood, on account of the roads south being watched too closely for anyone's comfort these days. Saddlebags, guarded, within the week."

Marian's expression did the thing it always did when a genuinely useful piece of information arrived — a kind of visible recalculation, plans reorganizing themselves behind her eyes almost faster than she could voice them.

"Then we don't need to go to Ashcombe at all," she said. "We need to be waiting on the road when it comes to us."

"How many guards?" Long John asked.

"My brother wasn't certain. Enough to matter. Not enough, he thought, to feel entirely confident about it — the Sheriff's been stretched thin since—" Ellen hesitated, glancing between them with the particular uncertainty of a woman who'd heard rumors she wasn't sure were meant to be repeated in present company. "Since whatever's been happening out this way, that's made him so short-tempered lately."

"That would be us," said Will, with more pride than the moment strictly called for.

"Some of it's us," Marian corrected. "Some of it's Vane, embarrassing himself to anyone who'll listen. We should be grateful men like that rarely blame themselves for their own bad decisions."

---

**[PLATE 4 — Robin testing a new angle/idea with his bow, comic aside, while the group continues planning around him]**

While the others worked through the finer points — the road's best ambush point, how many days until the shipment moved, whether Ellen's brother could be trusted to send word when it actually departed — Robin had drifted, as he tended to under pressure, toward practicing something that had nothing to do with any of it and everything to do with feeling useful.

"If we're taking a moving target," he said, mostly to himself, nocking an arrow, "I ought to practice on one."

He loosed at a swinging lantern Long John had rigged up for exactly this purpose. He missed the lantern by a comfortable margin and instead clipped the rope holding a sack of flour above the supply table, which descended directly onto Will with a soft, comprehensive *whumf*.

"I don't see how that helps the plan," Will said, entirely white from the collarbone up.

"It doesn't," Robin admitted. "But it wasn't nothing, either."

---

*(new text page)*

"He's not wrong," said Thistlewood, mildly, from where he'd been sitting mostly quiet at the edge of the discussion, Pip beside him equally content to listen rather than steer. "A man who can hit something he's not aiming at with that consistency is not, strictly, a man without skill. Merely one whose skill has taken an unconventional shape."

"That's very kind," said Robin, "and also not remotely reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be reassuring," said Thistlewood. "It was meant to be *accurate.*"

Marian, watching this exchange with the same quiet attention she'd been paying Thistlewood for weeks now, filed the comment away — not because it was strange on its own, but because it was, upon reflection, exactly the kind of thing that kept sounding less like a compliment and more like a man who understood Robin's aim rather better than a stranger reasonably should.

---

**[PLATE 5 — the plan solidifying, the gang and animals gathered together, forward momentum, closing on readiness]**

"Three days, then," Marian said, once the shape of it had finally settled — the ambush point, the timing, who'd take which position, what to do if Ellen's brother's estimate on the guard count turned out optimistic. "We'll want to be in place a day early, in case they move faster than word can reach us."

"I'll send what warning I can," Ellen said, already turning to go, some of the exhaustion in her easing now that the thing had been handed to people who might actually do something with it. "And — thank you. Whatever comes of it. It's more than Ashcombe's had from anyone in a long while."

"Don't thank us yet," said Marian, not unkindly. "Thank us once the coin's actually back where it belongs."

Once Ellen and the boys had gone, and the fire had burned down to something low and companionable, Grimalkin abandoned Marian's lap long enough to settle beside Gideon in the last of the daylight, tail flicking with the particular satisfaction of an animal who had spent an entire afternoon being proven right about everything he'd predicted.

*"Another one,"* he said. *"Third village in as many months. They're getting a reputation, our lot.*"

*"Good,"* said Gideon. *"Reputation's useful. Reputation's why that woman walked half a day instead of simply giving up."*

*"You've gotten sentimental,"* said Grimalkin, *"in your old age.*"

*"I've gotten accurate,"* said Gideon, which — from a goat who had, within recent memory, personally routed a dozen armed men — nobody present was inclined to argue with.
