# Story 7: The Ferret's Gambit
## Part Three: Empty Hands, Open Questions

**[PLATE 1 — tension building: signs the agent's suspicion has escalated, the crew and Tam preparing for worse]**

The warning, when it came, arrived considerably faster than any of them would have preferred — Wat and Hob again, breathless and grim, reporting that the tanner's shop had been watched openly since the previous evening, men stationed at both ends of the street with the particular unsubtle patience of people who no longer cared whether their presence was noticed.

Robin, peering carefully through the same cracked shutter Tam had used the night before, could make out two of them clearly — one leaning against the baker's wall across the way with the studied casualness of a man pretending to wait for bread, the other simply standing, unhurried, watching the tannery's door with the flat patience of someone entirely comfortable being seen.

"They're not hiding it anymore," Tam said, beside him, voice tight. "That means whatever suspicion your friend planted yesterday, it's grown into something they intend to act on properly."

"How much is left to find?" Marian asked, already recalculating.

"Nothing incriminating," Tam said. "We saw to that. But a search conducted by men who've already decided to be suspicious tends to find suspicion regardless of what's actually there to discover."

"Then we let them search," Marian said, after a moment's consideration, "and we make certain there's genuinely nothing left to find — not evidence hidden imperfectly, but a cellar so thoroughly ordinary that suspicion has nowhere left to attach itself." She looked around at the gathered group. "We finish the job properly this time. Every ledger, every crate, gone or genuinely innocent by morning."

"And if they ask questions neither of us can safely answer?" Tam asked, quietly, watching Will in particular.

"Then we answer only what's true," Robin said, "and nothing more than that. It's served us well enough so far."

Outside, the watcher at the baker's wall shifted his weight, glanced once toward the tannery's shuttered windows, and settled back into his patient vigil, entirely unaware of how much quiet, deliberate work was about to unfold on the other side of them before morning.

---

**[PLATE 2 — the overnight preparation: transforming the cellar into genuine, boring innocence]**

They worked through the night by shielded lamplight, the particular meticulous labor of people building not a hiding place but an absence — every remaining crate opened, inventoried, and either emptied entirely or restocked with goods so mundane that no amount of suspicion could make them interesting. Bolts of undyed wool where finer contraband cloth had sat the day before. Ordinary tallow candles replacing a crate of considerably rarer imported wax. Nothing hidden. Everything, simply, made boring.

"You're not concealing anything," Marian explained, watching Tam frown at an emptied shelf with the particular grief of a trader watching his actual livelihood vanish for the sake of appearances. "You're making certain there's nothing left worth concealing. It's a different kind of safety, but it's considerably more durable than a clever hiding spot."

"I understand the theory," Tam said, mournfully. "I'm simply allowed to mourn the profit margins while I agree with it."

Brother Crumb, meanwhile, had taken it upon himself to scrub the cellar's actual floor with real, dedicated thoroughness, on the theory — stated with total confidence and no particular evidence — that a floor too clean would look exactly as suspicious as one too dirty, and the correct answer lay somewhere carefully in between.

"You've given this considerably more thought than I expected," Will observed, watching him work.

"I've hidden things from an Abbot for fifteen years," Crumb said, without looking up. "One learns, eventually, precisely how much order looks honest and how much looks rehearsed."

By the time grey dawn light began creeping through the shutter's cracks, the cellar that greeted it was, by every visible measure, exactly what it claimed to be — the unremarkable stock of an unremarkable, entirely legitimate trader, with nothing beneath its surface but the considerable, exhausted effort it had taken to make it look that way.

---

**[PLATE 3 — the raid itself: Crane's agents arriving in force to search the cellar]**

They came at dawn, four of them this time, led by the same thin, unassuming man from before, moving through the tanner's shop with the methodical thoroughness of men who had done this a great many times and expected, this time, to actually find something.

"Master Tam," the lead agent said, producing a paper with the particular unhurried formality of a man who enjoyed the ceremony of authority precisely because he so rarely needed to raise his voice to exercise it. "A proper search, this time. Witchfinder Crane's authority, should you require it stated plainly."

"By all means," Tam said, with a calm that cost him considerably more than he let show. "I've nothing to hide from a man doing his honest duty."

The search that followed was thorough, patient, and — to the private, considerable relief of everyone crouched in the tannery's upper rooms, listening — entirely fruitless. Crate after crate, opened and catalogued and found dull. The ledgers, examined page by careful page, revealing nothing but ordinary transactions in ordinary goods.

*"They're good at this,"* Grimalkin observed, low, watching from a rafter with professional appreciation despite himself. *"Thorough. I'll grant them that much."*

*"I don't like it,"* Barley said, pressed close beside him, uncharacteristically subdued. *"It doesn't feel like a game, this one."*

*"No,"* Grimalkin agreed. *"It rather doesn't."*

Below them, one of the searching men lifted the very bolt of undyed wool Marian had positioned the night before precisely where a more interesting fabric had sat, turned it over once with visible disinterest, and set it back down exactly where he'd found it — the small, unremarkable proof that a night's exhausting labor had done precisely what it was meant to.

---

**[PLATE 4 — the interrogation taking a dangerous turn: pointed questions connecting to the witchcraft rumors]**

It was only once the physical search had concluded, empty-handed, that the true danger of the morning revealed itself — the lead agent, notebook out, settling into the particular unhurried patience that Robin recognized, with a cold jolt, as identical to the Sheriff's own manner during his own interrogation months before.

"You deal with a great many people, Master Tam," the agent said. "Traders. Travelers. I wonder whether you've heard anything of an old man, out Sherwood way. Keeps unusual company. A grandson who fights rather better than any grandson ought to. Animals that seem, by more than one account, considerably more capable than animals generally are."

Tam's face did not change, though Marian, watching closely, saw the effort it cost him to keep it that way. "Can't say I have," he said, easily. "I deal in goods, not gossip."

"A pity," the agent said, unbothered, closing his notebook with the particular unhurried finality that suggested the conversation had cost him nothing regardless of the answer. "Witchfinder Crane has developed rather a comprehensive interest in the subject. He believes — and I've learned not to doubt him on matters like this — that the truth of it is considerably closer to the surface than most people assume. He simply requires patience, and enough small pieces, to see the whole shape of it eventually."

He left without further ceremony, his men falling in behind him, and it was a long while after the door had finally closed that anyone in the tannery's upper rooms felt it safe to properly breathe again.

"He's not guessing," Marian said, quietly, once the street outside had emptied. "He's assembling. One conversation at a time."

---

**[PLATE 5 — the immediate crisis resolved, the group processing what they've just survived]**

"We're safe, for today," Tam said, once the shock had settled enough to permit ordinary speech again, surveying his hollowed-out, thoroughly ransacked, thoroughly innocent cellar with the particular exhaustion of a man calculating exactly how long rebuilding his actual stock would take him. "Whatever else that was, it wasn't an arrest, and my cellar's clean enough now to survive a second look if he sends one."

"Today," Marian repeated, with real emphasis on the word. "That man's patient in a way that should worry all of us considerably more than it currently seems to."

Will, who had said very little through the whole ordeal, finally spoke, his voice carrying none of its usual ease. "He asked about an old man with a grandson who fights too well. He's not simply fishing anymore, Marian. He's describing something specific. Someone's told him enough to sketch the shape of it, even if he hasn't got the whole picture yet."

---

**[PLATE 6 — closing: real dread settling underneath the day's narrow success, Marian's private unease]**

Nobody had a comfortable answer to that, and nobody pretended otherwise.

"We got Tam through today," Robin said, eventually, into the settling quiet. "That's not nothing. Whatever comes of Crane's patience, it isn't coming today, and today's rather all any of us can properly manage to fight for at a time like this."

"Today," Tam agreed, with feeling, already beginning the considerable work of restoring some order to his ransacked but empty cellar. "I'll take today, gladly, and worry about tomorrow once it's actually arrived to be worried about."

---

*(new text page)*

Marian said nothing further for a long while, helping Tam quietly restock what could be restocked, her hands steady even as her mind turned over, with real and growing unease, everything the morning had actually revealed.

An old man. A grandson who fought too well. Animals more capable than animals ought to be.

It was, she realized, watching the last of the useless wool bolts being carried back to wherever it had come from, no longer a question of whether Crane would eventually assemble the whole shape of the truth from pieces like these. It was only a question of how much time that assembly would actually take him — and whether it would be considerably less than any of them were currently prepared for.
