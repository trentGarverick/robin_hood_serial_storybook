# Story: Six Degrees of Sherwood
## Part Two: Long John Little

*(Working title — happy to change.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — Robin and Long John making a rough camp for the night, firelight, forest]**

They walked until the light started to go soft and orange, and then a while longer than that, on the general theory — unspoken but mutually agreed upon — that neither of them particularly wanted to be the one who suggested stopping first. It was Long John's stomach that eventually settled the matter, with a growl loud enough that a nearby bird left the tree it had been sitting in.

"Sorry," said Long John, to the bird.

They made a fire. Long John made it, specifically, in about a third of the time it would have taken Robin, with the unhurried competence of a man who has built a great many fires and rather fewer of anything else that had gone well for him lately.

"Can I ask you something," Robin said, once the fire had settled into itself.

"You're going to ask why a guard's got a blacksmith's hands," said Long John. It wasn't a question.

---

**[PLATE 2 — flashback begins: a thriving village forge, Long John (younger, unbearded or differently groomed to suggest "before") at work, warm and prosperous]**

Which it was, and which brings us — because there is no elegant way to arrive at a man's worst year except to simply arrive at it — to a forge on the northern edge of a village smaller than the one with the geese, where Long John Little had, for some years, been the only smith worth the name for eight miles in any direction.

It had been, by his own account, a good trade. Plough blades. Horseshoes. The occasional sword, though those were rarer and he didn't much like making them, on the grounds that a blade's whole purpose was a thing he'd rather not think too hard about while his hands were on it. People paid him in coin when they had it and in favors, eggs, and firewood when they didn't, and for a good while this had all balanced out the way these things do in villages too small to bother with anything as formal as bookkeeping.

Then the tax rose. Not sharply — nothing so honest as that — but steadily, the way water rises in a boat with a slow leak, a little more each season, until the people who used to pay Long John in coin were paying it in nothing at all, because there wasn't any nothing at all left over once the King's due and the Sheriff's due and whatever due came after that had all had their turn.

---

**[PLATE 3 — flashback: the forge empty and cold, Long John alone, a small worn cottage in the background implying a mother he cares for]**

"Couldn't fault them for it," Long John said, back at the fire, in the tone of a man who had spent a great deal of time and effort not faulting anyone for it. "Man's got to feed his own before he pays another man to shoe his horse. I understood that. Still do."

What he had not been able to do was eat understanding, or feed it to his mother, who was old and getting older and lived in the cottage behind the forge and had opinions — strong ones, frequently — about a son of hers taking the King's coin to stand in a square and watch other people's geese get repossessed. He had not told her, in the end, what the job actually was. He had told her it was "guard work," which was true in the way that "adventure" is true of falling down a well.

"Candle wax," he said, half to himself, half to the fire. "That's what did it. Not the man himself — just the candle wax, season on season, till there was nothing left to smith for."

---

**[PLATE 4 — present day: Robin looking puzzled by the phrase, Long John mid-explanation, firelight scene continuing]**

Robin frowned in the exact way he had been frowning at cant for as long as he'd been hearing it, which was to say: politely, and with absolutely no comprehension whatsoever. "Candle wax did what, precisely? Melted your tools?"

"It means *tax*, Robin."

"Then why not simply say tax?"

"I have genuinely started to wonder that myself," said Long John, "and I have decided I don't want to know the answer, because I suspect it will not be satisfying." He prodded the fire. "Point is. Took the coin because the coin was there and the forge wasn't, not anymore, not enough of it. Didn't enjoy a day of it. You saw how well I took to it."

"You knocked over a pottery stall and apologized to a cart."

"I stand by both of those," said Long John, with dignity.

---

**[PLATE 5 — the tax man's search party arriving through the trees at night, torches, alarmed and angry, closing in on the camp]**

It was at this point — and Robin would later reflect that this was, in fact, always the point at which things happened — that the trees to the north produced torchlight, several raised voices, and the general unmistakable sound of men who have been sent to find someone and have just realized they might actually be about to succeed.

"That'll be the tax man," said Long John, standing with the particular speed of a very large person deciding, all at once, to stop being at a campfire.

"Are you certain?"

"I am fairly certain, yes, on account of the fact that one of them is shouting my name."

Robin, given no time to construct a plan of the usual four-second variety, reached instead for his bow, which — in fairness to him — was at this point simply what his hands did when they had nothing better to be doing.

---

**[PLATE 6 — chaotic skirmish: Robin's arrow going astray again, Long John accidentally toppling something onto the searchers, both of them working together despite the chaos]**

What followed did not resemble, in any particular, a fight. Robin loosed an arrow at approximately a search party and hit, with the same gentle indifference to planning that had served him so well in the village square, a low branch directly above the nearest man's head, which dropped a startled owl into the proceedings at a velocity and volume that no one present had been prepared for. Long John, attempting to place himself protectively between Robin and the torchlight, instead placed himself into a stack of deadwood he had gathered earlier for the fire, which came down around the two nearest searchers with the comprehensive suddenness of a small, localized avalanche.

"Sorry," said Long John, to the deadwood, and then, reconsidering, to the men currently buried under it. "Sorry — didn't mean — are you quite alright under there?"

They were, as it turned out, extremely disinclined to answer that question, being significantly more concerned with the owl.

---

**[PLATE 7 — Robin and Long John fleeing the scene, and stumbling upon Will Scarlett-Sleeves comically stuck/caught in some predicament nearby — a hook into Part Three]**

They ran, again — Robin was beginning to suspect this was simply going to be a theme — and did not stop until the shouting had thinned to nothing behind them and the forest had thickened to something close to true dark ahead. It was in this dark, some considerable distance later, that they nearly walked directly into a young man hanging upside down from a low branch by one ankle, entirely unbothered by his circumstances, in the middle of what appeared to be a long and detailed argument with himself about whether his sleeve had been damaged.

"Ah," said the young man, spotting them, entirely unruffled by being spotted while upside down. "Might one of you have a knife? Or, failing that, a considerable amount of patience? I find myself between engagements."

Robin looked at Long John. Long John looked at Robin.

"I feel," said Long John, with the weary calm of a man revising his expectations for the evening downward yet again, "that we ought to ask him his name before we cut him down. Purely so we know who to send the bill to."
