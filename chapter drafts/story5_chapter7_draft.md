# Story 5: The Sheriff's Reckoning
## Part Seven: The Line Held

*(Working title — happy to change. The major confrontation of this 8-part story.)*

**[PLATE 1 — Ellen arriving with urgent warning: Blackwood plans retaliation against Ashcombe]**

Ellen arrived at a dead run, two days after the rescue, with news that settled over the camp like a physical weight.

"Blackwood's furious," she said, catching her breath. "Losing Robin — losing him from inside her own Keep — it's made her look incompetent to the Sheriff, and she means to make an example of somewhere to prove she isn't. My brother heard it from a soldier who'd had too much ale. Ashcombe. Because it's the village that's helped you most visibly. She means to burn what she can't find."

The camp went very quiet.

"When?" Marian asked, already standing.

"Two days. Perhaps three."

"Then we don't wait for her to arrive," Robin said, and there was something in his voice — steadier, harder than it had been even a week ago — that made everyone present look at him properly. "We've spent all of this reacting. Scouting them. Fleeing them. Being caught by them. I'm done with reacting."

"You're proposing we attack a professional military operation," Will said, "on purpose."

"I'm proposing we defend a village that helped us," Robin said, "before it burns because of us. There's a difference, and I'd rather be remembered for making it than for hiding from the consequences of not making it."

Marian looked at him for a long moment, something like real pride settling behind her usual careful assessment.

"Agreed," she said. "Let's plan properly, this time."

---

**[PLATE 2 — the crew and allies preparing Ashcombe's defense, rallying resources, a real sense of unity and purpose]**

They had two days, and they used every hour of them — Long John and Will fortifying the approaches with whatever the village could spare, Brother Crumb organizing the townsfolk into something resembling order, Thistlewood and Pip working through tactics with Marian late into both nights, the animals scouting the approach roads for any sign of Blackwood's advance.

Ashcombe itself, for its part, did not hesitate. Ellen's neighbors — the same people who'd once simply endured whatever the Sheriff's men demanded of them — took up whatever tools came to hand with the particular fierce determination of people who had finally been given something worth actively defending rather than merely surviving.

"They didn't have to help," Robin said, watching an elderly farmer sharpen a scythe with grim purpose. "They're choosing to."

"That's rather the point of everything we've been doing," Marian said. "You just hadn't seen it from this angle before."

---

*(new text page)*

Gideon returned from the approach road at a dead run late the second night, no theatrics wasted this time, alert and staying alert in a way that bore no resemblance to his usual unhurried reports.

Marian couldn't have told anyone what he'd actually meant by any of it, but the sheer, uncharacteristic urgency in his return — no pause, no dry commentary, simply arriving and staying tense until she'd properly registered him — told her everything she needed regardless.

"Whatever's coming," she said, to the assembled crew, "it's coming sooner than we planned for. We finish tonight, not tomorrow." She looked around at the gathered defenders — crew and villagers both — and found, in their steady, determined faces, something that settled the last of her own uncertainty. "Let's be ready."

"Understood," said Will, checking his sleeve one final time with real, focused intent rather than his usual idle habit.

---

**[PLATE 3 — Blackwood's forces arriving, the confrontation beginning, real tension and stakes]**

Blackwood's column arrived at midday, disciplined and unhurried as ever, and found — instead of an undefended village ripe for burning — a line of Ashcombe's own people standing shoulder to shoulder with five outlaws and an old man with a staff, blocking the road with a resolve that visibly, briefly, gave even Blackwood's practiced composure pause.

Vane rode near the column's rear, visible the moment the ranks parted, and Marian felt something in her settle into cold, familiar certainty at the sight of him.

"This is not your fight," Blackwood called out, to the assembled defenders, in the flat, carrying voice of a woman entirely unaccustomed to being defied. "Stand aside, and no one here needs to be hurt today."

"It became our fight," Marian called back, steady, "the moment you decided to burn people for helping us. We're rather fond of them. We'd like you to leave."

---

*(new text page)*

"You're outnumbered," Blackwood said, though something in her voice suggested she was recalculating, in real time, exactly how outnumbered either side actually was.

"We're outnumbered," Marian agreed. "We're not out-prepared. There's a difference, and I'd recommend you consider it before this goes any further than words."

Vane, from behind Blackwood's line, couldn't quite contain himself any longer. "Marian Fletcher," he called, with real venom. "Still playing at soldiers with your little band of thieves. I do hope you're prepared to lose considerably more than a strongbox this time."

"Vane," Marian said, flat, unimpressed. "Still confusing volume for competence, I see."

---

**[PLATE 4 — the battle breaking out, real stakes, the crew and villagers fighting together, showcasing their combined skill]**

The fighting, when it broke, had none of the loose comic chaos that had carried them through so much of this — this was defense, deliberate and coordinated, Long John and the sturdier villagers holding the road's narrow choke point while Will and Marian worked the flanks, Thistlewood's staff finding wrists and knees with the same careful, deniable precision he'd always shown, Pip moving through the chaos with an openness she'd never quite allowed herself in front of strangers before.

Brother Crumb, for his part, had apparently decided that his pie-throwing skills merited a wider application, and was distributing considerably less friendly projectiles with the same devastating accuracy he'd shown at the Keep.

"I really must ask you your secret with that arm sometime," Long John called, ducking a soldier's blade.

"Buy me a drink first," Crumb called back. "A man doesn't give away all his mysteries for free."

---

*(new text page)*

Marian found Vane exactly where she'd expected to — hanging back from the actual fighting, as he always did, directing rather than risking himself — and crossed the distance between them with grim, unhurried purpose.

"You've had this coming for a while," she said.

"I've had a great many things coming," Vane said, backing away, some of his earlier bravado thinning considerably now that the confrontation had stopped being theoretical. "Doesn't mean I intend to simply accept delivery."

"You don't get a vote in that," Marian said, and closed the distance before he could finish backing away, disarming him with the same brisk efficiency she'd once used on a strongbox — no cruelty in it, simply the calm certainty of a debt finally being properly settled.

---

**[PLATE 5 — Blackwood recognizing the battle isn't going her way, tactical retreat, real respect exchanged despite the conflict]**

Blackwood, watching her carefully calculated advantage erode with every passing minute against a defense considerably better organized than she'd accounted for, made the decision every genuinely competent commander eventually has to make — the one that costs pride rather than lives.

"Fall back," she called, clear and carrying, no shame in her voice at the order, only the flat professional acceptance of a woman who understood the difference between a battle worth losing and a war worth continuing. "We're done here today."

Her soldiers withdrew in disciplined order, no rout, no panic, simply a controlled retreat that spoke, even in defeat, to exactly the competence that had made her dangerous in the first place.

---

*(new text page)*

Blackwood herself lingered a moment longer than her retreating men, meeting Marian's gaze across the settling battlefield with something that wasn't quite respect and wasn't quite hostility either.

"You're better organized than I gave you credit for," she said.

"You're more honest than most of the Sheriff's men we've met," Marian returned. "I'd almost call that a compliment, if the circumstances were different."

"Almost," Blackwood agreed, and turned to follow her column without further ceremony, leaving behind her a battlefield that belonged, however temporarily, entirely to the people who'd fought to keep it.

---

**[PLATE 6 — closing: victory, but bittersweet — Vane dealt with, Blackwood retreated but not defeated, the Sheriff and Crane still looming]**

They bound Vane securely, once the dust had settled, and it was Ellen — of all people, given everything Ashcombe had suffered — who suggested, with quiet, practical justice, precisely what ought to be done with him.

"Take him to the Sheriff yourself," she said. "Let his own patron see exactly how well his information served him. I doubt the Sheriff's especially forgiving of failures that embarrass him publicly."

"That," said Marian, with real satisfaction, "is a considerably better punishment than anything we could arrange ourselves."

Vane, hearing this, went a shade paler than his usual prosperous complexion allowed for, and offered, for once, no clever retort at all.

---

*(new text page)*

That evening, once Ashcombe's wounded had been tended and its dead — mercifully few — properly mourned, the crew sat together at the village's edge, tired in the particular satisfied way that only follows a fight actually worth having.

"We won," Will said, testing the word like it might not quite fit.

"We won today," Marian corrected, gently. "Blackwood retreated — she wasn't defeated, and she'll be back, more careful next time. Vane's dealt with, but the Sheriff still sits in Nottingham, and Crane's still asking his questions somewhere we can't see him." She looked around the fire at all of them, weary and whole and together. "But today, a village that helped us still stands, and that's not nothing. That's rather the whole point, actually."

Robin, watching the fire, said little for a long while — some quiet, unfinished thought from a stone cell and a patient voice still turning over in him, unresolved, though he kept it, for now, entirely to himself.
