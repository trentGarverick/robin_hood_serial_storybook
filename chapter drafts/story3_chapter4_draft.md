# Story 3: The Devil's Own Luck
## Part Four: A Word in Private

*(Working title — happy to change. Closing chapter of Story 3.)*

**[PLATE 1 — the warm return to camp, animals greeting the gang enthusiastically, celebratory mood]**

They returned to Sherwood two days later, tired in the particular satisfied way that only follows a genuinely difficult thing gone right, and were met at the camp's edge by a reception considerably more enthusiastic than five exhausted outlaws strictly deserved.

Barley reached them first, predictably, at a dead sprint that ended in Long John being personally and enthusiastically reintroduced to the ground. Gideon offered a more measured welcome, though even he abandoned his usual composure long enough to headbutt Will's leg with what could only be described as affection, provided one was willing to be generous about interpreting goat behavior as affection at all.

"I've missed you too," Will said, with feeling, wobbling slightly. "Gently, perhaps. Gently would be appreciated."

"He's not being gentle *at* you," said Grimalkin, from his customary perch on Marian's shoulders, already resettled as though he'd never once considered vacating the position. "He's being gentle in general terms, which for Gideon is roughly the same thing as flowers."

"That's a low bar," said Will.

"He's a goat," said Grimalkin. "The bar was never going to be especially high."

Marian, laughing despite herself for the first time in what felt like several exhausting days, reached up to scratch behind Grimalkin's ears without quite thinking about it, in the easy unconscious way one does for something that has, somewhere along the way, stopped needing to be a decision at all.

---

**[PLATE 2 — continued celebration: Robin's playful bow demonstration, warm camaraderie, a small Marian/Robin moment]**

Robin, riding the general good mood, had taken it upon himself to demonstrate — with more confidence than the demonstration strictly earned — exactly how he'd taken the helmet clean off a guard's head without touching the man beneath it, a feat which, attempted a second time for an audience, produced an arrow that sailed cheerfully over the practice target entirely and vanished into the undergrowth with a soft, unceremonious rustle.

"In fairness," he said, into the ensuing silence, "I never claimed I could do it *twice*."

"Nobody's disappointed," Marian said, and there was something in the way she said it — warmer than the words alone accounted for — that made Robin glance at her a beat longer than the joke really required, though neither of them remarked on it, and the moment passed the way such moments generally did between the two of them: noticed, unspoken, filed away for some later day that neither was in any particular hurry to arrive at.

Brother Crumb, meanwhile, had produced enough food to feed twice their number, which surprised no one, and was distributing it with the particular satisfaction of a man who considered a successful job properly finished only once everyone present had eaten their fill of something that wasn't travel rations.

"A hound's-tooth of a job, that was," he said, settling onto a log with his own plate. "Hardest thing we've done, and the truest good to come of it."

Robin blinked. "A hound's—"

"*Truth*, Robin," said the whole gathered camp, animals and all — or at any rate, three distinct voices pitched in the animals' general direction that Robin, charitably, assumed were simply echoes.

---

**[PLATE 3 — Thistlewood arriving, expression serious, breaking the celebratory mood]**

It was Thistlewood's arrival, some while later, that finally settled the evening into something quieter. He came without Pip this time, walking stick and all, wearing an expression none of them had seen on him before — not his usual mild exasperation, but something closer to genuine concern, carefully controlled.

"I'd hoped to let you enjoy the evening a little longer," he said, without preamble, "but I think you'd rather have this now than wonder later why I waited."

"How do you even hear these things," Will asked, only half joking, "all the way out here?"

"Old habits," said Thistlewood, which answered nothing at all and closed the subject regardless. Marian, watching him with the same quiet attention she always did now, noted — as she noted most things about him these days — that this was, itself, a rather convenient sort of vagueness.

"The Sheriff knows what happened to his deputies' shipment," Thistlewood said. "He knew within a day, which tells you something about how closely he's been watching this road since the manor business. He's called his captains in. He's spoken, apparently at some volume, about being made a fool of three times in as many months, and about intending that it not happen a fourth."

"That's not exactly specific," Robin said.

"No," Thistlewood agreed. "But men like that rarely announce specifics before they act. They announce *intent*, and let the specifics arrive as a surprise to everyone but themselves. I'd take the intent seriously, if I were any of you. I rather think I am, in fact, taking it seriously on your behalf, which is presumably why I walked all this way to say so."

---

**[PLATE 4 — the gang's varied reactions to the news: concern, dark humor, processing together]**

The mood that settled over the camp afterward was not exactly fear, though it wasn't nothing close to it either — Long John went quiet in the particular way that meant he was doing arithmetic he didn't like, Will's hand found his sleeve and stayed there rather longer than usual, and Brother Crumb set down his plate for the second time that evening, which everyone present had learned, by now, to recognize as a genuinely serious sign.

"Well," said Marian, eventually, into the settling quiet, in the brisk tone of a woman refusing to let dread have the last word if she had anything to say about it. "We knew this was coming, one way or another. We've simply been given the courtesy of a little warning before it does."

"That's one way to look at it," said Robin.

"It's the only useful way to look at it," Marian said. "The other way just means sitting here being frightened instead of ready. I know which one saves lives."

"For what it's worth," Thistlewood added, more gently, "you've earned rather more good fortune than bad, across all of this. I wouldn't spend tonight assuming that changes simply because a man in Nottingham is currently very cross about it."

"Easy for you to say," Will muttered, "you're not the one he's cross *at*."

Thistlewood's expression did something complicated at that — brief, and gone before anyone but Marian thought to look closely enough to catch it — before he inclined his head and said only, mildly, "No. I suppose I'm not," in a tone that Marian, alone among them, found herself turning over for rather longer than the sentence seemed to warrant.

---

**[PLATE 5 — closing: Pip quietly approaching Marian away from the others, asking to speak privately]**

It was well after full dark, once the camp had settled into the low, subdued quiet of people who'd been given something serious to think about and were mostly failing to think about anything else, that Pip found Marian alone at the edge of the firelight, refilling a water skin with more concentration than the task strictly required.

"Might I have a word," Pip said, quietly, glancing once toward the others before returning his attention fully to her. "Somewhere a bit more private than this."

Marian set the water skin down slowly, some old, patient certainty in her settling at last into something that felt less like waiting and more like arrival.

"Alright," she said, and meant it in every sense the word could carry. "Lead the way."
