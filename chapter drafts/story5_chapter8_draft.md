# Story 5: The Sheriff's Reckoning
## Part Eight: The Quiet After

**[PLATE 1 — warmth: the full team resting and celebrating together back at Sherwood Camp, genuine relief and camaraderie]**

They stayed at Ashcombe two more days, helping where hands were still needed, before finally making the long walk home to Sherwood — and it was only once the camp's familiar fire was properly lit, once Barley had finished his enthusiastic third reunion of the week with Long John, that the whole ordeal finally seemed to settle into something they'd actually survived rather than something still happening to them.

"I keep waiting for the next disaster," Will admitted, stretching out with real, unguarded contentment for what felt like the first time in weeks. "It's rather unsettling, not having one immediately at hand."

"Give it a week," said Marian, dry, though even she allowed herself something close to a proper smile.

*(new text page)*

Vane had been handed off to a passing patrol two days prior, bound and thoroughly chastened, with a brief note — composed by Marian with real, careful satisfaction — explaining precisely whose failure he represented and to whom that failure properly belonged. What became of him afterward, none of them knew, and none of them, if they were honest, felt especially inclined to spend much time wondering.

"Good riddance," Brother Crumb said, with the particular finality of a man closing a book he'd never especially enjoyed reading.

"Good riddance," the whole camp agreed, and for once, nobody felt the need to say anything more about it.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Robin finding a quiet moment alone with Marian, away from the others, the emotional core of the chapter beginning]**

It was Robin who sought Marian out, later that evening, finding her at the camp's edge sharpening an arrow with the same careful attention she gave most things that actually mattered to her.

"Can I ask you something," he said, sitting down beside her without quite waiting for permission, "and have you not tell me I'm imagining things?"

Marian didn't look up from her work, though something in her posture went very slightly careful. "You can ask."

"Since the Keep," Robin said, slowly, choosing each word with more care than he usually bothered to. "I keep thinking about something the Sheriff said. About luck that isn't quite luck. And I keep — I don't know. Noticing things. Small things. Things I never used to let myself properly look at."

---

*(new text page)*

"What sort of things," Marian asked, carefully neutral, setting the arrow down.

"I don't entirely know how to say it without sounding like a man who's spent too long alone in a cold cell," Robin admitted, with a small, rueful laugh that didn't quite reach the rest of him. "Just — things go right for us. More often than they reasonably should. And I've always simply been grateful for it, because questioning good fortune felt like tempting it to leave. But now I can't quite stop questioning it, and I don't know what I'm meant to do with that."

Marian was quiet for a long moment, turning over, with real and genuine care, exactly how much of the truth a person could offer without offering the truth itself.

---

**[PLATE 3 — Marian's careful, warm response, dramatic irony, real emotional connection without revealing anything]**

"I think," she said, finally, "that some things in this life don't owe you a full explanation simply because you've noticed them. I think you can trust something — trust the people around you, trust the shape your life has taken — without needing to understand every piece of how it got that way." She held his gaze, steady, warm, carrying considerably more honesty in it than the careful words alone let on. "For what it's worth, Robin — whatever's been looking out for you, all this time. I don't think it means you any harm. I think, if anything, it means you rather a lot of good, by people who care what happens to you more than you've ever properly let yourself imagine."

"That's not really an answer," Robin said, though gently, without any real complaint in it.

"No," Marian agreed. "It's the truest thing I can give you tonight, though. I hope it's enough, for now."

---

*(new text page)*

"It's enough," Robin said, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he meant it — not because the uncertainty had actually resolved into anything more solid, but because something in the way she'd said it, the care she'd so plainly taken with every word, had settled something in him that the words alone couldn't quite account for.

"Thank you," he added, "for not laughing at me. Or telling me I'm imagining it."

"You're not imagining it," Marian said, simply, and there was nothing careful in that particular sentence at all — just plain, unguarded truth, offered because it was the one piece she could give him whole. "Whatever else I can or can't tell you, Robin, believe that much. You're not imagining any of it."

They sat together a while longer, comfortable in a silence that held considerably more understanding in it than either of them had quite expected going in.

---

**[PLATE 4 — the wider camp settling into peace, other threads gently touched — Ashcombe's recovery, Ellen, the crew's bond deepened]**

Word reached them, over the following days, that Ashcombe was mending well — Ellen's own message, carried by Wat and Hob with considerably more cheer than their last few urgent errands had allowed for, reporting rebuilt fences, a properly celebrated harvest, and a town that had, in its own quiet way, come out the other side of the fight rather prouder than it had gone in.

"They're calling it a victory," Hob reported, delighted. "*Your* victory. Ellen says half the town wants to name something after you, though nobody can agree what."

"Please, anything but a well," Will said, with real feeling. "We've had quite enough of wells this month."

---

*(new text page)*

Thistlewood and Pip stayed close through those quieter days, the three-way understanding between them and Marian settling further into something none of the rest of the crew fully questioned anymore, simply accepted the way one accepts weather — present, occasionally inconvenient, generally for the good.

"You did well," Thistlewood told Marian, quietly, one evening, watching Robin across the fire, laughing at something Will had said, easy and unguarded in a way that suggested the conversation from days before had genuinely helped rather than simply been endured. "Whatever you told him."

"I told him the truth," Marian said. "Just not all of it. I'm not certain that's entirely the same thing as lying, but I've decided not to examine that too closely for now."

---

**[PLATE 5 — closing: a hook/tension note — word of Crane's continued investigation, or the Sheriff's reaction, keeping real stakes alive]**

It was Ellen, in the end, who brought the final piece of news — arriving one last time, not urgent this time, simply thorough, the way she'd always been.

"The Sheriff's furious about Vane," she said. "Properly furious, by all accounts — losing a source that expensive, that publicly, hasn't improved his temper any. But there's something else. The Witchfinder. Crane. He hasn't stopped asking his questions, even with everything else that's happened. If anything, Ashcombe's given him more to work with, not less. More witnesses. More stories to compare against each other."

Marian's expression settled into something careful and considering. "He's patient. That was always the dangerous part about him."

---

*(new text page)*

That night, once the camp had quieted and most of them had settled toward sleep, Marian sat a while longer by the dying fire, turning the day's news over with the same careful attention she gave everything that actually mattered.

Vane, dealt with. Blackwood, retreated but not defeated, certain to return more carefully next time. The Sheriff, humiliated and furious in Nottingham, already planning whatever came next. And Crane — patient, methodical, still quietly gathering the exact pieces of truth that mattered most, one careful interview at a time.

*One thing at a time,* she told herself, the way she'd told herself a great many times this year, and found, as she generally did, that it was still the only sensible way to carry a weight this size.

Somewhere behind her, the fire settled with a soft, contented crack, and Sherwood, for one more quiet night at least, kept its secrets safe.
