# Story 3: The Devil's Own Luck
## Part Three: A Convenient Accident

**[PLATE 1 — animals left behind at camp, watching the gang depart, banter over the departure]**

The gang left before first light, which meant the animals were left to their own devices considerably earlier than any of them would have preferred, a fact Barley made known by sitting at the camp's edge and staring after the departing party with an expression of profound betrayal.

"They'll be back," said Gideon, not looking up from his breakfast.

"You don't know that."

"I know they always have been, so far. That's not nothing." Gideon considered this further, chewing thoughtfully. "Besides. He's going, isn't he. So they'll be fine."

Grimalkin's tail flicked with interest. "You noticed that too."

"Hard not to," said Gideon. "Man doesn't announce he's *not* going anywhere nearly as often as he has lately, unless he's going somewhere."

*"Do you think they know?"* Barley asked, abandoning his vigil at the treeline to flop down beside the other two with the particular gloom of a dog denied an adventure. *"That he follows?"*

*"The clever one suspects,"* said Grimalkin. *"The rest haven't the faintest idea. Which is rather the point of him, I'd imagine.*"

*"Seems like a lot of trouble," said Barley, "just to help without anyone noticing."*

*"Some people,"* said Gideon, with the air of a creature who had given this considerably more thought than anyone had asked him to, *"prefer it that way. Doesn't mean it costs them any less."*

---

**[PLATE 2 — the gang in position along the forest road, tense waiting, Robin fidgeting with his bow]**

They'd found their spot the evening before, a bend in the road narrow enough to funnel a cart through single-file and treed closely enough on both sides to hide five people who had, by now, gotten reasonably competent at not being seen when it mattered. Waiting, Robin had long since decided, was the worst part of any job — worse than the running, worse than the danger, simply because it left too much room in a man's head for his own thoughts.

He restrung his bow for the third time in an hour, more out of nervous habit than any actual concern for the string.

"You're going to wear a groove in that thing," Marian observed, not looking up from where she was sharpening an already-sharp knife with the same restless energy.

"I could say the same to you."

"You could," she agreed. "It wouldn't stop either of us."

Will, for his part, had taken the waiting considerably better, mostly by treating it as an opportunity to conduct a lengthy, entirely one-sided debate with himself about whether his current sleeve had reached the end of its natural life and warranted retirement. Brother Crumb had produced food from somewhere, as he always did, and was distributing it with the unhurried calm of a man who considered any crisis improved by a proper breakfast regardless of the hour. Long John simply sat, enormous and still, with the particular patience of a man who had learned, the hard way, that his size made stillness considerably more valuable than pacing.

"Cart's coming," Long John said, eventually, in a voice pitched low enough to carry exactly as far as it needed to and no further.

---

**[PLATE 3 — the shipment arriving with more guards than expected, the gang's alarm]**

It was not, as it turned out, a modest operation. Where Ellen's brother had guessed at guards "enough to matter," the reality proved to be considerably more than that — eight men at least, riding close formation around a heavily laden cart, with the disciplined watchfulness of soldiers who had been specifically warned to expect trouble on this stretch of road.

"That's not what we planned for," Will murmured.

"No," Marian agreed, already recalculating, "it isn't." She took a slow breath, the particular steadying kind she reserved for moments that had just become considerably more dangerous than advertised. "Positions. We adapt. We don't panic."

"Nobody's panicking," said Robin, in the tone of a man very much on the edge of panicking.

"Good," said Marian. "Keep it that way."

The ambush, when it came, had rather less finesse than any of them would have preferred and rather more volume — Robin's opening shot took a guard's helmet clean off without touching the man beneath it, which did nothing for their tactical position but did wonders for morale, and Long John's arrival among the cart's escort had the immediate, comprehensive effect of a boulder introducing itself to a stream. Guards scattered, regrouped, scattered again. It was, by the low standards the day had already set, going about as well as eight-against-five ever could.

Then one of them broke free of the fighting entirely, spurred his horse hard toward the road's northern bend, and was gone before anyone could properly register what it meant.

---

**[PLATE 4 — a guard/rider breaking away to raise the alarm, real danger, the gang's reaction]**

"He's for Nottingham," Marian said, already moving, already understanding exactly what it meant if that particular man reached exactly that particular destination. "If he reaches the garrison before we're clear of this road, it isn't five outlaws they'll be looking for anymore. It's an army."

Robin was already running, bow in hand, though even he understood, somewhere past the adrenaline, that a man on horseback had no business being caught by a man on foot, however good his luck occasionally proved to be. The gap between them widened with every stride. The rider didn't so much as glance back.

"I can't reach him," Robin said, breathless, already slowing, already doing the arithmetic that said this was a race already lost.

And then, with no warning and no visible cause anyone present could later agree upon, the rider's horse stumbled.

It was not a dramatic stumble — no root visibly in the road, no obstacle anyone could point to afterward with any confidence — simply a single missed step at precisely the wrong moment, enough to unseat a man riding at full gallop and send him tumbling hard into the verge, winded and considerably more interested in his own bruises than in continuing his errand.

Robin reached him a few moments later, considerably more confused than triumphant. "That was— did anyone else—"

"Don't," said Marian, arriving beside him, already binding the fallen rider's wrists with brisk, practiced efficiency. "Don't ask. Just be grateful."

She said it lightly. It did not, this time, entirely reach her eyes.

---

**[PLATE 5 — the ambush concluding, coin secured, the gang regrouping, exhausted but successful]**

The rest of it went quickly once the alarm had been contained — guards who had lost their nerve alongside their numbers proved considerably easier to persuade than guards operating at full confidence, and the cart's contents, once properly counted, made for a satisfying enough haul that even Will's ongoing sleeve deliberations paused long enough for him to whistle low in appreciation.

"Ashcombe's due a very good week," he said.

"Ashcombe's due considerably more than a week," said Marian, already directing the coin's careful division — a portion set aside plainly and clearly for the town itself, the rest distributed with the same fastidious restraint she'd shown at Vane's strongbox. "But it's a start."

---

*(new text page)*

They delivered the greater share to Ashcombe within two days' hard travel — a brief, warmly received business that none of them, in the exhausted aftermath of the ambush itself, had much energy left to properly savor, though Ellen's face when she understood what had actually been accomplished was, Robin privately thought, worth the whole exhausting errand on its own.

It was on the walk back, well clear of both Ashcombe and the road, that Marian finally allowed herself to think properly about the horse.

She had turned it over a dozen ways already — a startled hare, an unlucky root, simple bad fortune meeting a man riding too hard for the ground beneath him — and none of it sat right, not stacked up against everything else she'd been quietly filing away for months now. The staff. The aim. The boy who moved like a soldier. And now a horse that stumbled at precisely, impossibly, the correct moment, with no visible cause and no witness willing to claim one.

She did not say any of this aloud. She simply walked, and thought, and let the certainty settle into something she no longer needed to question — only, for now, to carry quietly, the way she carried most things that mattered.
