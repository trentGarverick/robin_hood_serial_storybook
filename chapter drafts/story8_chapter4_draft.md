# Story 8: The Witchfinder's Patience
## Part Four: Too Clever By Half

**[PLATE 1 — word arrives that Crane visited the hermit, initial cautious relief]**

The word reached them two days later, carried by one of Tam's usual quiet contacts, and it was Marian who read the report first, her expression settling through several careful stages before arriving anywhere close to relief.

"He went," she said, finally, to the assembled group. "Out to the eastern district, exactly as we'd hoped. Spent the better part of a day with the hermit, by all accounts. Questions, observation, the same patient thoroughness he's shown everywhere else."

"Then it worked," Will said, allowing himself, for the first time in days, something like genuine hope.

"It bought us time," Marian corrected, carefully. "Whether it *worked*, in the sense we actually needed it to, is rather a separate question, and not one I'm prepared to answer with only half a report in front of me."

"What did he conclude?" Robin asked. "About the hermit, I mean."

"Nothing supernatural," Marian said, reading further. "The man's simply odd, same as he's always been, same as everyone already knew. Crane seems to have satisfied himself of that much fairly quickly." She paused, frowning at something in the report that clearly troubled her considerably more than the conclusion itself. "But there's a detail here I don't like the shape of. Apparently he asked the hermit, quite directly, how the rumors about him had started. When they'd first begun circulating. Who'd first mentioned his name to a stranger."

"That's rather specific," Long John said, uneasy.

"It's precisely the question a man asks," Marian said, "when he suspects a rumor didn't arise naturally at all."

---

**[PLATE 2 — the twist: Crane suspects manipulation, treating it as confirmation rather than deception]**

The fuller account, when it arrived the following day, confirmed exactly the fear Marian's careful reading had already anticipated.

"He's not fooled," she said, flatly, setting down the second report with considerably less composure than she generally permitted herself. "Not in the way we'd hoped, at least. He noticed the timing. Three separate conversations, all pointing the same direction, all arriving within days of his visit to Thistlewood — that's not coincidence to a man who's spent his whole career learning to distrust convenient coincidences."

"So he knows we planted it," Pip said, grim, having arrived partway through the report with Thistlewood close behind her.

"He suspects it," Marian corrected. "Which is, in its own way, considerably worse than simple deception would have been. A false lead that fooled him would have cost him time and nothing more. A false lead he *recognizes* as deliberate—"

"—tells him someone's actively protecting whatever he's investigating," Thistlewood finished, quietly, the particular weary clarity of a man who had turned this exact possibility over privately long before anyone else in the room had said it aloud. "Which means he no longer needs to wonder whether there's something worth finding. He simply needs to work out who's determined enough to interfere with his search, and follow that thread back to wherever it actually leads."

The silence that settled over the group carried real, uncomfortable weight to it — the particular stillness of people recognizing that a plan meant to buy safety had, instead, handed their adversary a genuinely useful new piece of the puzzle.

"We made it worse," Robin said, quietly, guilt plain in his voice. "Trying to help, and we made it worse."

---

**[PLATE 3 — the crew grappling with the backfire, reassessing without despair]**

"We didn't make it worse," Marian said, firm, though the correction cost her something to deliver with proper conviction. "We bought three days he didn't otherwise have. Three days we've used, however imperfectly, to prepare rather than simply wait. That has value, even if the plan's second effect wasn't the one we intended."

"It's a difficult thing to feel grateful for," Will said, "a mistake that happens to buy time alongside the damage."

"Most useful things in this life are difficult to feel simply grateful for," Thistlewood said, with the particular dry weariness of a man who had learned that lesson considerably more times than he'd have preferred. "That doesn't make the time less real, or less worth having properly used."

"What does he do now," Pip asked, "knowing what he knows?"

"I don't know precisely," Marian admitted. "But I'd wager he doesn't return to idle questions. A man who suspects deliberate interference stops treating his investigation as a curiosity and starts treating it as a proper contest. He'll come at this more directly next time. More prepared for resistance. Considerably less patient about being managed."

"Then we'd best be considerably more prepared ourselves," Robin said, resolve settling back into his voice despite the weight of the afternoon's news. "Whatever comes next, we don't face it caught as flat-footed as we were the first time he knocked on that door."

---

**[PLATE 4 — closing: the group bracing together, real tension but not despair]**

They sat together a long while as the light faded, the particular quiet that follows genuinely difficult news once the first shock of it has properly settled — not despair, exactly, but something considerably more sober than the cautious hope they'd carried into the day.

"For what it's worth," Pip said, eventually, breaking the silence, "I'd rather face a man who respects what he's chasing than one who dismisses it as nonsense. Crane taking us seriously is dangerous. But it's an honest kind of dangerous, at least. I've dealt with worse."

"That's either genuine wisdom," Will said, "or the darkest thing anyone's said all evening. I confess I can't quite tell which."

"Both, probably," Pip said, and something in the exchange — small, ordinary, entirely unforced — did more to settle the gathered unease than any amount of careful strategizing had managed all afternoon.

Marian, watching the fire settle into embers, found herself turning the whole shape of the danger over one final time before permitting herself the rest the day had earned everyone.

Three days bought, imperfectly. A patient man now hunting not merely a rumor but the hand that had tried to redirect him. And somewhere beyond tonight's fragile quiet, a contest properly begun in earnest, whether any of them had fully wanted it to be or not.

"Rest tonight," she said, to the gathered group, with the particular steady authority that had, by now, become simply how they all understood a genuine crisis to be handled. "Tomorrow, we plan properly for a man who's stopped being merely curious about us. That's a different fight than the one we've been managing so far, and I intend for us to meet it considerably better prepared than we've managed anything else this year."

---

**[PLATE 5 — levity: Gideon nudges Will off balance, sending him crashing into Robin mid-archery-practice, comedic chaos, animal commentary, Crumb cooking obliviously]**

The evening might have settled into properly solemn quiet after that, had Gideon not chosen precisely that moment to wander over and deliver, with what could only be described as deliberate affection, a firm nudge directly into the back of Will's knees.

Will, caught entirely off balance, staggered sideways with a startled yelp, arms windmilling in a valiant but doomed effort to recover his footing, and collided — with the particular comprehensive misfortune the universe seemed to reserve specifically for him — directly into Robin, who had chosen that same unfortunate moment to loose an arrow at the evening's last remaining light.

The arrow went nowhere useful whatsoever. Will went down in an undignified tangle of limbs and wounded pride. Robin, knocked sideways, sat down hard in the dirt with an expression of profound betrayal aimed squarely at the universe in general.

*"That was magnificent,"* Grimalkin observed, from a safe and considered distance, tail curling with real satisfaction. *"I don't believe I've seen him executed with quite that level of precision before. He's improving."*

*"He didn't mean anything by it,"* Barley said, loyally, watching Gideon amble away with the serene, unbothered dignity of a creature entirely unconcerned with the consequences of his own actions. *"Probably."*

*"He absolutely meant something by it,"* Grimalkin said. *"I simply admire that he's stopped pretending otherwise."*

"I am *covered* in dirt," Will announced, to no one in particular, examining the ruin of his sleeve with real anguish. "This is a genuine tragedy. Someone ought to be held accountable."

"The goat," Robin said, from where he was still sitting, "is right there."

Brother Crumb, entirely unbothered by the chaos unfolding a few feet from his cooking fire, simply added another log to the flames and stirred his pot with the placid contentment of a man who had long since stopped being surprised by anything this particular camp produced. "Supper in a quarter hour," he announced, to the group at large, "regardless of how many of you are presently on the ground."
