# Story: Six Degrees of Sherwood
## Part Five: Marian Fletcher

*(Working title — happy to change.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — the four of them approaching a modest fletcher's workshop in a village, arrows and bowstaves visible through the open front]**

Finding "a woman clever enough to keep the three of you alive" turned out to be less of a search than any of them expected, mostly because Brother Crumb appeared to already know exactly where to look, and led them there with the unhurried confidence of a man walking toward a meal he'd had before and enjoyed.

"Fletcher's daughter," he said, by way of explanation, as they approached a modest workshop with arrows fanned out in the window like a display of teeth. "Runs the place herself now. Best bowyer for two counties, and considerably cleverer than the trade strictly requires."

"You know her," Robin said.

"I know *of* her," Brother Crumb corrected. "Difference matters. You'll see."

---

They saw, in fact, almost immediately, because the scene through the open front of the shop was not one of quiet craftsmanship but of a woman conducting two entirely separate transactions at once with the seamless efficiency of someone who had stopped finding this difficult years ago.

To a threadbare farmer she was saying, "No charge for the fletching, just the shaft — call it a slow week," in a voice pitched to carry no further than the counter. To a bored-looking soldier in the Sheriff's colors, inspecting a bundle of arrows with the cursory attention of a man who has never once been refused anything, she was saying, at completely normal volume, "Finest ash, straight-grained, you won't find better this side of Nottingham," while wrapping a bundle that Robin — watching closely, and rather proud of himself for noticing anything at all — was fairly sure contained at least two shafts with a barely visible warp along the grain.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Marian handling both customers simultaneously, sharp and composed, the crew watching from the doorway]**

The soldier paid, took his flawed arrows, and left without once suspecting he'd been handed anything but the best the shop had to offer. The farmer left considerably lighter of debt and heavier of arrows, with the particular relief of a man who has just been quietly done a kindness and isn't going to embarrass either of them by mentioning it aloud.

Marian turned to the four strangers now filling her doorway, took the full measure of them in about the time it takes to blink, and said, without any particular surprise: "You're the ones who've been making the Sheriff's men look like fools all month."

"We prefer 'lucky,'" said Will.

"I prefer accurate," said Marian. "Which one of you is bleeding, and which of you is about to be, because I don't extend credit to strangers and I can already tell that's going to come up."

---

*(new text page)*

"None of us are bleeding," Robin offered, in the tone of a man aware this was a low bar to be proud of clearing.

"Give it time," said Marian, not unkindly, already turning back to her workbench. "Crumb. You're thinner than you were."

"I've been sharing," said Brother Crumb, with the air of a man reporting a minor scandal about himself.

"That'll do it." She glanced at the other three, cataloguing them with the brisk efficiency of a woman doing inventory. "An archer who can't aim, a giant who apologizes to furniture, and a man checking his sleeve in the middle of being sized up by a stranger with a knife rack behind her." Will, notably, stopped checking his sleeve. "I've heard the stories. I assumed they were exaggerated for effect."

"They weren't," said Long John, with real feeling.

"No," Marian agreed, looking at them for a long moment. "I don't imagine they were."

---

**[PLATE 3 — Marian explaining her own situation while continuing to work — fletching an arrow with practiced hands, unbothered]**

"Your turn," she said, hands never stopping their work — fitting fletching to a shaft with the unconscious precision of a decade's practice. "Why is a fletcher's shop the first stop for four wanted men?"

"Brother Crumb's idea," said Robin.

"Brother Crumb's *good* idea," Crumb corrected, settling onto a stool with the confidence of a man who'd clearly done this before. "You've been doing this a while, haven't you. The soldier and the farmer. That wasn't the first time."

Marian's hands didn't slow, but something in her face went carefully, deliberately neutral — the specific stillness of someone deciding how much truth a stranger has earned. "My father ran this shop honestly," she said eventually, "right up until honest stopped being something the tax collectors left room for. He's gone now. I kept the shop. I did not," she added, with a precision that made clear this was a distinction she'd drawn for herself more than once, "keep the honesty. Not entirely. Not where it wasn't earning its keep."

*(new text page)*

"The soldiers never notice," Will said, with the professional appreciation of a man recognizing a fellow craftsman. "The warp in the grain."

"They notice eventually," said Marian. "By then it's an afternoon's ride away and difficult to prove it was deliberate. Wood warps. Everyone knows wood warps." She held up the arrow she'd been finishing, sighted down its length with one eye closed, apparently satisfied. "It's not much. A weak bowstring here, an ounce of coin skimmed there. I never fooled myself it was more than that. Small mercies, is all — a hound's-tooth's worth of resistance, and no more."

Robin blinked. "A hound's what?"

"*Truth*, Robin," said all three of the others, in a unison so immediate and so weary that even Marian — who had known him for perhaps a quarter of an hour — looked faintly impressed by the sheer consistency of it.

"I begin to see why you travel as a group," she said. "Someone has to translate."

---

**[PLATE 4 — a quieter, more direct exchange — Marian looking at the four of them with real assessment, considering something]**

"You could do more than small mercies," Robin said, "with us."

"I've heard what 'with us' looks like," Marian said, not unkindly, gesturing vaguely at the general shape of the four of them — Long John, who had somehow already knocked a bundle of dowels askew simply by existing near them; Will, once again checking his sleeve; Brother Crumb, quietly eating something he had produced from a pocket. "Forgive me if it doesn't inspire immediate confidence."

"It shouldn't," Robin admitted. "We're not — I won't pretend we're good at this. Any of it. We've mostly been lucky."

"Mostly," Marian repeated, with the particular emphasis of a woman filing a word away for later examination.

---

*(new text page)*

"But the tax man's crew, the collectors, the abbey — none of that was luck alone," Robin pressed on, with more conviction than he'd managed all week. "Somewhere in it, we actually helped people. The Hale family got their stool back. You've been doing the same thing, alone, with warped arrows and quiet coin. I'm only asking whether it wouldn't go further — whether *you* wouldn't go further — with people willing to stand next to you while you did it."

Marian set down her tools, properly, for the first time since they'd arrived, and looked at him with an attention that made Robin distinctly aware of how little of it he usually earned.

"That," she said slowly, "was almost a good argument."

"I have my moments."

"Rarely," said Marian, "but apparently not never."

---

**[PLATE 5 — Marian closing up the shop, gathering a few essential belongings, the five of them now assembled together for the first time]**

She was quiet for a while after that, finishing the arrow in her hands with the unhurried thoroughness of someone unwilling to leave a piece of work half-done, even one that no longer mattered nearly as much as the conversation happening around it. When she finally set it aside, it was with the air of a decision already made rather than one still being weighed.

"I'll want my own tools," she said, already moving toward a locked chest behind the counter. "And I'll want it understood, from the start, that I am not doing this because four strangers with a lucky streak wandered into my shop and asked nicely."

"Then why?" asked Long John, gently.

"Because I've been doing this alone for two years," said Marian, "and I am, if I'm honest with myself, very tired of it being small." She latched the chest, shouldered it, and looked at the four of them — really looked, in the particular way of someone taking on a weight and deciding, deliberately, that she was willing to carry it. "Try not to make me regret this within the week."

"No promises," said Will, cheerfully.

"That," said Marian, "is exactly the answer I was afraid of."

---

*(new text page)*

They left the shop together, five now instead of four, in a configuration that felt — to Robin, walking slightly ahead and trying not to make a show of noticing it — different in some way he didn't have quite the right word for. Not safer, necessarily. Long John still walked like a controlled collapse waiting for a reason. Will still checked his sleeve at unhelpful intervals. But something had settled that hadn't been settled before, the particular feeling of a wobbling table finally finding its fourth leg.

"So," said Marian, falling into step beside them, already surveying the road ahead with the assessing eye of a woman used to spotting trouble before it spotted her. "Where exactly are we headed, and how much of the plan is actually a plan?"

There was a pause.

"We're working on that part," said Robin.

"Of course you are," said Marian, and did not sound even slightly surprised.
