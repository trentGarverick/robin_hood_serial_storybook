# Story 10: A Father's Reckoning
## Part One: A Name Remembered

*(Working titles — happy to change either. First of a 7-part finale — this is the last story of Book 1. Please read carefully.)*

**[PLATE 1 — Captain Blackwood conducting deeper investigation into Robin specifically, methodical and thorough]**

Captain Blackwood had spent the months since Ashcombe doing precisely what she'd promised — not licking her wounds, not simply waiting for a second chance to present itself, but working, patiently and methodically, toward making certain her next encounter with this particular band of outlaws bore no resemblance whatsoever to her last one.

She had started, as she always started, with the man at the center of it — Robin of Loxby, by the name he went by now, though her deeper inquiries had turned up considerably more history than a simple outlaw's name generally carried with it.

"Loxby," she said, aloud, to the small stack of records and inquiries she'd assembled across weeks of careful, unhurried digging. "A minor holding, north of the county. A family that fell rather suddenly out of prominence, some years back, under circumstances nobody in the district seemed especially eager to discuss."

The pieces, once she'd properly assembled them, formed a picture considerably more specific than she'd expected to find — a father who'd once held modest standing, a debt of some significant size incurred and never fully accounted for, a son who'd vanished from the district not long after, and a family name that hadn't crossed any official record since.

Blackwood was not, by nature, a woman given to satisfaction over incomplete work, but she permitted herself a small measure of it now, recognizing the shape of something genuinely useful forming in her hands.

"This," she said, to herself, gathering the papers with brisk, professional care, "is rather more than I expected to find. I believe the Sheriff will want to hear this personally, and rather sooner than later."

---

**[PLATE 2 — Blackwood bringing the information to the Sheriff, his reaction shifting from professional interest to something personal]**

The Sheriff received her report in his usual unhurried manner, reading through her careful notes with the particular patient attention he brought to anything that might genuinely matter, right up until his eyes reached the name itself — and something in his composure, for the first time in Blackwood's considerable experience serving under him, visibly, unmistakably shifted.

"Loxby," he said, quiet, something old and complicated working behind his usually unreadable expression. "I know this name."

"I rather thought you might, given how thoroughly the county records seemed to want to forget it," Blackwood said. "A debt of some significant size, my inquiries suggest. Owed, if I've assembled the pieces correctly, to your own office."

"To me personally," the Sheriff said, and there was something in his voice now that Blackwood had genuinely never heard from him before — not simply calculation, but the particular old, careful weight of a man revisiting a debt considerably older than the coin it had originally represented.

---

*(new text page)*

"The outlaw who's cost us so dearly this past year," the Sheriff said, slowly, working through the shape of it with real, private focus, "is the very same son who vanished from Loxby's holding some years past. I confess I hadn't made the connection until this precise moment. The world, I find, has rather a talent for arranging its debts to come due at exactly the moment one least expects them."

"Does this change our approach?" Blackwood asked, professionally, watching him with careful attention.

"It changes everything about our approach," the Sheriff said, setting the papers down with deliberate, unhurried finality. "I find I have rather more reason to resolve this matter properly than simple professional embarrassment ever gave me. Prepare your forces, Captain. Quietly, and considerably more thoroughly than last time. I believe it's finally time to collect on an old debt of my own."

---

**[PLATE 3 — meanwhile: Robin's father, through his own remaining channels, learning the Sheriff now knows]**

Some considerable distance from Nottingham, in a modest home that had seen better years, a man well past his middle years received news that settled over him with the particular cold weight of a fear he'd spent a very long time quietly managing.

"The Sheriff's been asking after Loxby," his old steward reported, breathless from the ride, the last loyal servant of a household that had once employed considerably more than one. "Making inquiries. Quiet ones, but thorough. I've a cousin still working the county records — she says a Captain came through, asking questions nobody's asked in years."

The man closed his eyes briefly, the particular exhausted stillness of someone absorbing news they'd long dreaded without ever quite believing it would actually arrive. "They've found him," he said, quiet. "After everything. After all this careful distance. They've finally found my son."

---

*(new text page)*

"What will you do?" the steward asked, watching him with real, long-standing concern.

The man was quiet a long moment, some old, difficult calculation working behind features that had, Robin would scarcely have recognized, aged considerably harder than the years alone should have accounted for.

"I've spent years keeping my distance," he said, finally, "telling myself it was the safest thing I could offer him, given everything I'd already cost him simply by being his father. I find I no longer believe that's true. Not if the Sheriff's men reach him with their own explanation before I've had the chance to offer mine." He stood, with the particular grim resolve of a man who had finally run out of safer alternatives. "Ready the horse. Whatever this costs me, my son deserves to hear the truth from me directly, before it reaches him from considerably less kind quarters."

---

**[PLATE 4 — closing: the clock now running on both sides, real urgency established]**

The steward moved to comply, though something in his own weathered expression carried real, unspoken worry. "You understand what you're risking, going to him directly. The Sheriff's men are already moving."

"I understand precisely what I'm risking," the man said. "I've understood it for years, in the abstract. I find the particulars matter rather less to me now than they once did, given what's actually at stake." He paused at the door, something old and sorrowful and entirely resolved settling across his features. "I let my own fear keep us apart for too many years already. I don't intend to let it cost me the chance to finally set things right, whatever the Sheriff's men manage to arrange in the meantime."

He rode out within the hour, north toward Sherwood, toward a son who had no notion whatsoever that the careful, protective distance defining his entire adult life was about to close, all at once, from both directions simultaneously.
