**[PLATE 1 — finding Ferret Tam's current operation, his first proper on-page appearance since Ch3]**

They found him, eventually, in a cellar beneath an unremarkable tanner's shop, surrounded by crates he was in the process of sorting with the particular frantic inefficiency of a man trying to organize a crisis and mostly rearranging it instead.

Ferret Tam had not changed much in the years since Will's original debt — still wiry, still sharp-eyed, still possessed of the particular nervous energy of a man who had built an entire livelihood on angles other people hadn't thought to consider. He looked up at their arrival with an expression that moved rapidly from alarm to profound, undisguised relief.

"You actually came," he said. "I'll be honest, I gave it perhaps one chance in three."

"We don't leave people to it," Robin said. "Even people we've had disagreements with."

"Disagreement," Will repeated, dry, though there was real warmth underneath it. "That's a generous word for what I did to you, Tam."

"You owe me money," Tam said, matter-of-fact, "which is a considerably smaller crime than the one currently being investigated in my general direction, so let's agree to set it aside for the moment and return to it once neither of us is at risk of arrest."

"I intend to pay it," Will said. "Properly. Once this is settled."

Tam blinked at him, genuinely startled, and for a moment the frantic energy of the cellar seemed to pause entirely around that small, unexpected sincerity. "Well," he said, eventually. "Wasn't expecting that today, on top of everything else."

**[PLATE 2 — Tam explaining the full threat: goods, evidence, and his own safety all at risk]**

"Here's the shape of it," Tam said, once the introductions had settled and the crew had begun, without needing to be asked, sorting crates alongside him. "Crane's man came round twice now, asking after my dealings — polite about it, which is somehow worse than if he'd simply threatened me outright. I've got goods here that would raise considerably more questions than I'd like answered, records of who's bought what from whom, and my own continued freedom, roughly in that order of how badly I need to protect them."

"What kind of records?" Marian asked, already assessing the stacked ledgers with a professional eye.

"The kind that name names," Tam said, grim. "Yours included, here and there, if anyone thought to look closely enough. I'd rather they didn't get the chance."

"Then we move everything," Marian said, already reorganizing the cellar's chaos into something resembling a plan. "Goods somewhere Crane's man hasn't thought to look yet. Ledgers somewhere they can be properly destroyed, not simply hidden — hidden things get found eventually. And you somewhere considerably harder to locate than a tanner's cellar, at least until we understand exactly how much Crane actually knows."

"You're rather good at this," Tam observed, watching her work.

"We've had practice," Long John said, already hefting the first of the heavier crates with the particular ease of a man who found honest labor a welcome change from everything else this story had asked of him so far.

---

**[PLATE 3 — the relocation effort underway, comic tension mixed with genuine urgency]**

What followed had the particular chaotic efficiency of people working against a clock none of them could actually see, ledgers sorted and burned in careful batches, crates redistributed among three separate hiding places Tam swore by and Marian quietly improved upon, Brother Crumb — inexplicably, though nobody questioned it anymore — producing exactly the right kind of oilcloth to keep the remaining goods dry from somewhere in his robes.

"I don't suppose," Tam said, watching Crumb work, "you'd tell me where you keep finding all of this."

"No," Crumb said, pleasantly, and continued working.

Will, meanwhile, had fallen into an easy rhythm beside Tam that surprised them both — old business partners, however contentious the original arrangement, falling back into a shared language of practical cooperation neither had quite expected to still remember.

"I really am sorry," Will said, quietly, during a lull, "for how badly I handled things between us. I was young, and vain, and considerably more interested in appearances than in honesty, and you deserved better dealing from me than you got."

"You were young," Tam agreed, "and vain, and I was rather taking advantage of both those facts, if I'm honest about my own end of it. I don't imagine either of us covered ourselves in glory." He hefted another crate, considering. "Water under the bridge, mostly, at this point. Though I'll still expect that payment, mind, once we're all still breathing to discuss it properly."

"You'll have it," Will said. "You have my word this time, for whatever that's worth to you now."

"Rather more than it used to be," Tam admitted, "given the last hour."

---

**[PLATE 4 — a close call: one of Crane's agents arrives unexpectedly, tense improvisation, narrow escape]**

It was Grimalkin — who had, along with the other two, tagged along at a discreet distance without anyone quite deciding to invite them — who gave the first warning, a low, urgent stillness that Marian caught immediately even without understanding the words behind it.

*"Someone's coming,"* he said, to the other two, sharp. *"Moving careful. Not a guard's walk. Something quieter."*

Marian was moving before she'd fully processed her own instinct, gesturing the others into stillness with the brisk economy of a woman who'd learned to trust exactly this kind of warning without needing to know its source.

A moment later, the cellar door opened, and a thin, unassuming man in plain dark clothes — clearly not Crane himself, but cut from precisely the same careful cloth — stepped through with a notebook already in hand.

"Master Tam," the man said, mild, unhurried, in a voice that gave away nothing at all. "I'd hoped to catch you at home. I have a few further questions, if you've the time."

Tam, to his considerable credit, recovered his composure with a speed that suggested rather more practice at exactly this kind of moment than any of them had properly appreciated until now. "Always time for the Church's business," he said, smoothly, stepping forward to block the man's view of the half-emptied cellar behind him. "Though I confess my cellar's rather a mess just now — inventory, you understand. Terrible timing on your part. Shall we discuss this properly upstairs, where there's decent light and I can actually offer you something to drink?"

The agent's eyes lingered a moment too long on the shadows behind him, and Marian, pressed flat against the cellar wall with the others, held her breath until the door finally, mercifully, closed again.
