# Story 8: The Witchfinder's Patience
## Part Five: The Weight of Evidence

**[PLATE 1 — the crew and Thistlewood preparing together for Crane's next visit, planned defenses]**

They had three days' warning this time, courtesy of a note passed through Tam's careful network, and used every hour of it preparing in a way the previous visits had never properly allowed for.

"He'll come with something specific," Marian said, laying out the shape of their planning with her usual brisk precision. "Not idle questions this time. He's spent too long assembling pieces to arrive empty-handed at a confrontation he's clearly been building toward."

"Then we prepare for specifics," Thistlewood said, grim but steady. "Whatever he brings, we'll need an answer ready before he's finished asking the question — hesitation, more than anything else, is what confirms a suspicion faster than any evidence he could actually produce."

They rehearsed it properly, that evening and the next — every plausible question, every reasonable explanation, Marian pressing Thistlewood with the particular relentless precision of a woman determined to find every crack in a story before someone considerably less friendly had the chance to find it first.

"Where were you, the day Ashcombe was attacked?" she asked, for perhaps the fifth time, watching his answer with a critic's careful eye.

"Here," Thistlewood said, steady. "Tending my garden. I heard of it only afterward, same as any concerned neighbor might."

"Good," Marian said. "Say it exactly that plainly, whatever he actually shows you. Don't embellish. Embellishment is where lies come apart."

---

**[PLATE 2 — Crane's arrival, presenting the specific evidence directly]**

Crane arrived precisely on the third day, alone as always, and dispensed with pleasantries considerably faster than either of his previous visits had permitted.

"Master Thistlewood," he said, producing a small object from within his coat and setting it, without ceremony, on the table between them. "I wonder if you recognize this."

It was a button — plain, unremarkable at a glance, save for a distinctive carved pattern along its edge that Marian, watching from the shadowed doorway, recognized instantly with a cold lurch of dread. She had seen that exact pattern a dozen times, on the coat Thistlewood had worn during Ashcombe's defense.

"Should I?" Thistlewood asked, mild, though Marian could see, even from a distance, precisely what the question cost him to deliver so evenly.

"It was found near Ashcombe," Crane said, watching him with the particular unhurried attention of a man who had learned, over a long career, exactly how much silence a difficult question could productively hold. "The day of the fighting there. A witness described an old man with a staff, fighting alongside the defenders — considerably more capable than his apparent age should have allowed. This button was recovered from the field afterward, matched by two separate tailors to a coat of unusually specific make." He paused, deliberately. "I'm told you own a coat of remarkably similar description."

"Many men own coats," Thistlewood said, carefully. "Buttons are hardly rare enough to prove a man's presence anywhere in particular."

"No," Crane agreed. "They aren't. Which is precisely why I'm asking, rather than simply concluding."

---

**[PLATE 3 — the tense confrontation continuing, Thistlewood's careful deflection, real danger of the moment]**

"I was here," Thistlewood said, exactly as rehearsed, exactly as plainly as Marian had insisted. "Tending my garden, same as most days. I heard of Ashcombe only after the fact, from travelers passing this way. I'm sorry to disappoint whatever theory's brought you to my door with a button in hand, but I've no more connection to that day's fighting than any other concerned old man reading distant news."

"A great many coincidences have accumulated around you, Master Thistlewood," Crane said, quiet, unhurried, entirely unconvinced by the smoothness of the denial. "A staff wielded with unusual skill. A grandson who fights better than any grandson reasonably should. Animals more capable than animals generally are. And now a button, matched to your coat, found on a battlefield you claim never to have visited."

"Coincidences accumulate around a great many innocent people," Thistlewood said. "I'm sorry that mine have proven inconvenient to your theory."

"Perhaps," Crane said, and there was something in the careful, unhurried way he said it that carried considerably more weight than the single word alone should have. He studied Thistlewood a long moment longer, close and searching, before finally, deliberately, returning the button to his coat rather than leaving it on the table between them.

"I don't believe you," he said, plainly, without heat, simply stating a fact he'd apparently decided no longer needed softening. "I want to be clear about that, so there's no confusion between us going forward. I don't have proof yet. I'm not a man who acts without it. But I don't believe a word of what you've just told me, and I intend to keep looking until the proof and the belief finally meet somewhere in the middle."

He left without further ceremony, and it was a long, difficult while before anyone inside the cottage felt able to properly breathe again.

---

**[PLATE 4 — aftermath: the danger fully acknowledged, real cost of the confrontation]**

"That was close," Pip said, finally, emerging from her own hiding place with real, unhidden shock still visible on her face. "Considerably closer than the last visit. He's not simply suspicious anymore. He's *certain*, whatever he calls the gap between certainty and proof."

"He is," Thistlewood agreed, and there was something in his voice — old, weary, genuinely frightened in a way none of them had heard from him before — that unsettled the room considerably more than the confrontation itself had. "I've managed his patience for months now. I don't know how much longer I can manage his certainty."

Marian, still processing the full weight of what they'd just survived, found herself turning the danger over with grim, careful attention. "We bought today," she said. "That's not nothing. But we didn't buy tomorrow, or the day after. He's not leaving this alone. Not now."

"What happens," Robin asked, quietly, "if he finally gets his proof?"

Nobody answered that immediately, the question settling into the room with a weight none of them felt properly equipped to lift.

"I don't know," Thistlewood admitted, finally, honestly, stripped for once of his usual careful composure. "I've spent so long managing the question of *whether* he'd find the truth that I've given rather less thought than I should have to what actually happens once he does. I suppose we'll need to decide that, sooner rather than later, whether any of us are properly ready to." He looked around at the gathered group — tired, frightened, entirely unwilling to abandon him regardless — and found, in their steady presence, something that settled at least a portion of the fear properly. "For now, though. For tonight. I'd simply like to be grateful we're all still standing here to have the conversation at all."

---

**[PLATE 5 — closing: the weight of the near-miss settling in, resolve for what comes next]**

They stayed at the cottage that night, all of them, an unspoken agreement that nobody carrying this particular weight ought to carry it alone, not even for a single evening.

"We'll need a real plan," Marian said, quietly, to Thistlewood, once most of the others had finally settled toward uneasy sleep. "Not misdirection. Not careful denial. Something that actually accounts for a man who's already decided he doesn't believe us, whatever we say next."

"I know," Thistlewood said. "I've known for some while now, if I'm honest with myself about it. I simply hoped I'd have longer before needing to face it properly."

"None of us get quite as long as we'd like," Marian said, not unkindly. "We simply make do with the time we're actually given."

Thistlewood was quiet a long moment, watching the fire burn low, something old and considering working across his face that Marian, by now, had learned to recognize as the particular weight of a man turning over a decision he'd been avoiding for considerably longer than was comfortable to admit.

"There may be a way to end this properly," he said, finally, careful, testing the words as though speaking them aloud made the possibility more real than he was entirely prepared for. "Not misdirection. Not denial. Something considerably more direct, and considerably more dangerous, if it goes wrong."

Marian looked at him for a long moment, something in his expression telling her, before he'd said another word, that whatever he intended to propose would ask more of all of them than anything Crane's patient investigation had managed to demand so far.

"Tell me," she said.
