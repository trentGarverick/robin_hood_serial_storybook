# Story: Six Degrees of Sherwood
## Part Four: Brother Crumb

**[PLATE 1 — Brother Crumb waking up groggily, the three others gathered around, morning light]**

Brother Crumb woke the way he apparently did most things, which was slowly, contentedly, and with immediate concern for the whereabouts of food. He sat up, patted the flattened remains of the pie against his chest with something like grief, and looked at the three strangers now watching him with an expression of mild, unhurried interest, as though they were a somewhat unusual weather pattern rather than three people who had, within the last hour, fled two separate armed pursuits.

"Ah," he said. "Company. Lovely. Is there breakfast, or are we still at the stage of discussing whether there ought to be?"

"We hadn't got that far," Robin admitted.

"Then we've got that far now," said Brother Crumb, already on his feet and rummaging through a sack that appeared to contain, in defiance of every law of portability, considerably more food than a sack that size ought to.

Will watched this with the particular fascination of a man cataloguing a fellow professional. "That sack," he said, "should not fit what you're taking out of it."

"Faith," said Brother Crumb, without turning around.

"That's not an answer."

"No," Brother Crumb agreed cheerfully, "it isn't," and produced, with a flourish, a second, entirely separate pie, to a silence from the other three that could only be described as reverent.

Long John, who had by this point accepted a great many strange things about this particular day, decided this one didn't need investigating further, and simply held out his hands.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Brother Crumb cooking over the fire, clearly skilled and generous, portioning out real food for all four of them]**

What followed did a great deal to correct Robin's growing impression that the friar was simply a large, sleepy man who happened to travel with pastries. Brother Crumb cooked the way some men fought — with total confidence, an economy of motion that belied his size, and an apparent inability to make anything that wasn't, against all odds given the ingredients, genuinely good. He portioned it out generously, and evenly, and without once suggesting that he might like the largest share for himself, which surprised Robin more than the cooking did.

"You're better fed than any of us," Long John observed, around a mouthful that suggested he wasn't complaining.

"Comes of practice," said Brother Crumb. "Man learns to stretch a larder when he's spent years doing it for more mouths than his own."

Will, who had finished his portion with a speed that rather undercut his usual air of leisurely refinement, eyed the pot for a second helping with the naked hope of a man trying to appear as though he wasn't hoping. "You said fifteen years."

"Fifteen years," Brother Crumb agreed.

"That's a long time to be exceptionally good at something and only just now be showing off about it."

"I wasn't showing off," said Brother Crumb, spooning a second helping onto Will's plate without being asked, which rather undermined the point he was making. "I was feeding people who needed feeding. Showing off is a separate skill I have chosen, in my wisdom, not to develop."

"Could've fooled me," muttered Robin, eyeing the second pie.

"I heard that," said Brother Crumb, entirely without rancor, "and you're not wrong."

---

**[PLATE 3 — quieter scene at the fire, Crumb explaining his history with the abbey, more serious expression than before]**

"The abbey's larder, I take it," said Will, who had, over the last day, developed a habit of noticing the shape of a story before it was finished being told.

Brother Crumb was quiet for a moment — a genuinely unusual state for him, long enough that Robin began to wonder if he'd said something wrong.

"Kitchener," he said eventually. "Fifteen years. Good work, mostly. Except the larder was always fuller than the village was, and nobody at the abbey seemed to find that half so troubling as I did. Abbot's a decent man. Cautious man. Believed in charity the way you believe in saving for winter — a little, carefully, never so much you'd notice it was gone." He shrugged, a motion that on him had the gravity of a hillside settling. "I started finding it went missing rather more than a little. He started finding out. We had, let's say, a difference of opinion on the arithmetic of mercy."

"And?" Robin asked.

"And I decided the arithmetic worked better outside the walls," said Brother Crumb, "where I could do the giving myself instead of asking permission for it. Proper friar's-hood, that decision. Best I've made in years."

Robin frowned. "Friar's — you're a friar. Are you saying it was a friar's decision, or—"

"It means *good*, Robin," said Will, Long John, and Brother Crumb, in ragged, weary unison, and this time even Robin had the grace to look faintly embarrassed about it.

There was a pause after that, comfortable rather than awkward, the kind that settles over a fire once a true thing has been said and everyone's decided not to make a fuss of it.

"For what it's worth," Robin said eventually, looking at the fire rather than at Brother Crumb, "I understand it. The part where the people meant to look after you don't, quite, and you're left doing the sums yourself."

"Your father?" Brother Crumb asked, not unkindly, and not really as a question.

Robin didn't answer that directly, which was, in its own way, an answer. He prodded the fire instead, and after a moment Brother Crumb let it lie, in the manner of a man who understood exactly how much of a thing someone was willing to say, and had no intention of asking for more than that.

"Well," said Brother Crumb eventually, into the quiet. "We've all got an authority figure who thinks they know better, then. Comforting, in a way. Means we're in good company."

"Speak for yourself," said Will. "Mine's a debt collector."

"Same principle," said Brother Crumb. "Different invoice."

---

**[PLATE 4 — Abbot Percival arriving with two lay brothers, stern and disappointed expression, entering the camp]**

He did not, in the end, get much further than that particular satisfaction, because it was at this point that an elderly, portly figure in proper monastic robes emerged from the treeline, flanked by two younger brothers who had the distinct look of men who had been made to walk considerably further than they'd been trained for.

"Brother Crumb," said Abbot Percival, in the tone of a man who had rehearsed being calm about this and was already failing. "You are, I need hardly remind you, still nominally in my order."

"Abbot." Brother Crumb did not stand, but he did, at least, stop chewing. "You've come a fair distance to remind me of nominal things."

"I have come," said the Abbot, "because half the abbey's stores have been walking themselves to the poorer villages for the better part of a year, and I have only just this month worked out how, and I confess I am rather more hurt than angry, though I am also somewhat angry."

*(new text page)*

Behind him, the two lay brothers exchanged a glance with the weary solidarity of men who had walked a very long way to watch their superior have a difficult conversation. One of them, younger and considerably less composed about the whole affair, leaned toward the other.

"Is it true he took a whole side of bacon in one go?" he whispered, in the tone of a man asking about a folk legend rather than a colleague.

"Twice," the older one whispered back, with what might have been admiration.

"*I can hear you*," said the Abbot, without turning around, in the specific voice of a man who has spent decades pretending not to hear things that were said just slightly too loudly behind him.

---

**[PLATE 5 — the standoff: Crumb standing his ground for the first time, the other three (Robin, Long John, Will) quietly moving to stand with him]**

Brother Crumb got to his feet slowly, in the manner of a man who intended to take up rather more space in the conversation than he had so far.

"Then you've worked out I was doing good with it," he said. "Not selling it. Not eating more than my share — you can weigh me if you doubt that, though I'd rather you didn't. Giving it to people who needed it a great deal more than a locked cellar did."

"That was not your decision to make alone, Brother."

"No," Crumb agreed. "It was supposed to be *ours*, together, and it never once was, in fifteen years of asking. So I stopped asking."

There was a silence in which nobody moved, and then Robin — without entirely deciding to — stepped up beside him. Long John followed a half-second later, mostly by virtue of already standing nearby and not wanting to be the one left out. Will took slightly longer, on account of first checking his sleeve, but he got there too.

---

*(new text page)*

The Abbot looked at this new arrangement — his kitchener, flanked, apparently, by an archer, a giant, and a young man who was still fussing with his cuff even now — with the particular exhaustion of a man realizing he had walked into a negotiation he had not known was already lost.

"You've made friends," he said, in a tone that couldn't quite decide whether it was an accusation or an observation.

"Seems that way," said Brother Crumb, sounding faintly surprised by it himself, as if he were only now taking proper stock of the fact.

"They don't look like they can afford to feed themselves, let alone you."

"You'd be amazed," said Robin, "how far a bit of luck stretches."

The Abbot looked at him for a long moment, the way a man looks at a sentence he suspects means more than it appears to, and then, apparently deciding it wasn't worth pursuing, let it go.

---

**[PLATE 6 — Abbot Percival departing, exasperated but relenting, the four of them now clearly a unit, closing beat]**

"I am not," he said finally, "going to win this today."

"No," Brother Crumb agreed, not unkindly.

"I will be back," said the Abbot, with the weary dignity of a man setting a marker down rather than making a threat, and turned, and walked back the way he'd come, his two brothers trailing after him with the relieved haste of men who had not been looking forward to the walk anyway.

Brother Crumb watched him go, then sat back down and resumed eating, apparently unbothered, though he did — just once, when he thought no one was looking — glance after the Abbot with something considerably softer than relief.

---

*(new text page)*

"He'll actually be back, you understand," Brother Crumb added, to no one in particular, once the Abbot was well out of earshot. "Man's stubborn as a stump. It won't be the last of this."

"Does that trouble you?" Long John asked.

"Course it does," said Brother Crumb, entirely without heat, reaching for what remained of the second pie. "Doesn't mean I was wrong, though. A man can be troubled and correct at the same time. Happens more than people think."

"Well," he said, in conclusion, brushing crumbs from his robe with the air of a subject now thoroughly closed. "Where are we headed, then? I don't suppose any of you know where I might find a woman clever enough to keep the three of you alive. You'll want one. Trust me."
