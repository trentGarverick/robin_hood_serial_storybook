# Story 8: The Witchfinder's Patience
## Part Two: The Grandson in Question

**[PLATE 1 — inside the cottage, Thistlewood recounting the details of Crane's first visit]**

Thistlewood received them at his own hearth with a composure that cost him more than he let show, settling into his usual chair with the particular deliberate calm of a man determined not to let a crisis unsettle his manners along with everything else.

"He asked about herbs first," he said, once they'd all found somewhere to sit. "Remedies. Local knowledge — the sort of thing a scholar living this far out might reasonably be expected to have gathered. Perfectly ordinary questions, asked in a perfectly ordinary tone, which is precisely what made them so unsettling."

"What made them unsettling," Marian asked, "if they were ordinary?"

"Because he already knew the answers," Thistlewood said. "Every question was a test, not an inquiry. He wasn't asking to learn. He was asking to see whether I'd lie."

"Did you?" Robin asked.

"Sparingly," Thistlewood admitted. "Enough to protect what needed protecting, not enough, I hope, to give him anything solid to hold onto afterward. He asked how long I'd lived here. I told him longer than was strictly true. He asked whether I lived alone. I told him I had a grandson, which is, in the narrowest possible sense, not entirely a lie." He paused, something considerably more tired than his usual composure showing through for just a moment. "He looked at my staff twice, that I noticed. Didn't ask about it directly. I don't believe that was an oversight."

"He's building a case," Marian said, quiet. "Piece by piece, exactly as we feared."

---

**[PLATE 2 — word arrives that Crane is returning, urgent preparation]**

It was Pip who spotted him first, some while later, her attention drawn to the treeline by some small, practiced instinct none of the rest of them had quite developed yet.

"He's coming back," she said, low, already moving. "Alone this time, so far as I can tell. On foot, unhurried, the same measured pace as before."

"Today?" Will said, alarmed. "Already?"

"Patient men don't wait long between moves once they've started," Marian said, already thinking through the shape of what needed arranging in the few minutes they likely had left. "Everyone who shouldn't be seen, out of sight, now. Thistlewood, you know this game better than any of us. Tell us how to play our part in it properly."

"Stay hidden, all of you, unless I signal otherwise," Thistlewood said, already composing himself back into the mild, unremarkable scholar Crane had met once before. "And Pip—" He hesitated, something genuinely uncertain crossing his face for the first time since they'd arrived. "I rather suspect he means to ask after you directly, this time. He mentioned a grandson twice, when he questioned me. That's not idle curiosity. That's a man narrowing his search."

"Then he'll meet a grandson," Pip said, steady, already settling her cap more firmly, already letting the careful, boyish posture drop back over her like a coat pulled tight against real weather. "Same as he did the stories he's collected. Nothing more than that."

---

**[PLATE 3 — Crane's second visit beginning, more pointed than the first]**

Crane arrived precisely as unhurried as Pip had described, knocking with the particular measured patience of a man for whom time had never once been a pressure worth showing outwardly.

"Master Thistlewood," he said, when the door opened. "I hope I'm not imposing a second time."

"Not at all," Thistlewood said, warmly, entirely unbothered on the surface. "Though I confess I've little more wisdom to offer you than I did the first visit. My herb garden hasn't grown any more interesting in the interim."

"It isn't the herbs I've come about, this time," Crane said, mild, stepping inside without waiting to be properly invited. "I wondered whether I might meet your grandson. You mentioned him last visit. I find I'm curious about the sort of young man who chooses a life this quiet, this far from anywhere worth being seen."

"He's rather shy of strangers," Thistlewood said, easily, "but I'll see if he's willing." He raised his voice, calling toward the cottage's rear rooms with practiced casualness. "Pip! A visitor, if you'd be so kind."

Pip emerged a moment later, unhurried, boyish, entirely composed — the careful, practiced performance of a young man with nothing whatsoever to hide, offering Crane a brief, polite nod that gave away precisely nothing.

"Sir," Pip said, flat, disinterested, exactly the tone of a teenager unenthused about visitors.

Crane studied him for a long moment, close and unhurried, and Marian, watching from a gap in the rear doorway with her heart in her throat, found she could not, for the life of her, tell whether he'd seen anything at all.

---

**[PLATE 4 — the tense scrutiny continuing, Crane's pointed questions testing Pip directly]**

"You fought off highwaymen once, I understand," Crane said, conversational, notebook not yet out, though the absence felt, somehow, considerably more dangerous than its presence would have. "Rather well, by more than one account. Unusual, for someone your age."

"I had a good teacher," Pip said, with a small shrug, gesturing vaguely toward Thistlewood. "And considerable motivation. Men with swords tend to focus the mind."

"I imagine they do." Crane's gaze lingered, unhurried, cataloguing something Marian couldn't identify from her hiding place. "How old are you, if I might ask?"

"Fifteen," Pip said, without hesitation, the lie sitting as comfortable and practiced as everything else about the performance. "Sixteen come spring, my grandfather tells me, though I confess I've lost proper count myself."

"A common enough confusion, at your age," Crane said, and something in his tone — mild, unhurried, giving away absolutely nothing — made it impossible to tell whether he'd believed a single word of it or simply decided, for now, that pressing further wasn't worth the visible effort.

He didn't stay much longer after that — a few more idle questions, easily answered, before thanking them both for their patience and taking his leave with the same unhurried calm he'd arrived with, notebook never once produced, conclusions, if he'd drawn any, kept entirely to himself.

It was a long while after the door had finally, properly closed that anyone inside the cottage felt safe enough to breathe fully again.

---

**[PLATE 5 — closing: the visit survived, but real danger acknowledged, tension for what comes next]**

"That was closer than I'd like to admit," Pip said, finally, the careful performance dropping away all at once, leaving something considerably more shaken underneath it. "He wasn't simply asking questions. He was watching *everything*. My hands. My posture. How long I held his gaze before looking away."

"You did well," Thistlewood said, quietly, and there was real, unguarded pride in it. "Better than I'd have managed, at your age, under that particular kind of attention."

"I don't know that it matters how well I did," Pip said, "if he simply keeps coming back until something finally slips."

---

Marian, emerging from her hiding place at last, found herself turning the whole visit over with the particular careful attention she gave everything that actually mattered, and arrived, reluctantly, at the same conclusion she suspected everyone else in the room had already reached without saying it aloud.

"He'll come a third time," she said. "Perhaps not soon. But he will. A man that patient doesn't simply give up on a theory this close to confirmed." She looked around at all of them — Thistlewood, weary and unshakeable; Pip, still visibly rattled beneath her recovered composure; the rest of the crew, quietly absorbing the full weight of a danger none of their usual skills could properly out-fight. "We need a plan for when he does. Not simply hoping the performance holds a second time. A real one."

Nobody in the room had a comfortable answer to that yet. But nobody, watching Marian's expression settle into its familiar, determined focus, doubted for a moment that she intended to find one.
