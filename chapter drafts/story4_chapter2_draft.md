# Story 4: The Company She Keeps
## Part Two: A Watcher, Watched

*(Working title — happy to change.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — Gideon and the animals sensing something wrong near camp, alert and tense]**

It was Gideon who noticed first, same as always, though this time the noticing came with none of his usual flair for drama — no headbutting, no theatrical alarm, simply a sudden stillness mid-chew and a long look toward the eastern treeline that lasted rather longer than idle curiosity generally warranted.

*"Someone's been through there,"* he said, to the other two, low. *"Twice. Maybe three times. Not close enough to be careless about it, either.*"

*"Scouts?"* Barley asked, already low to the ground, all his usual bounce gone out of him.

*"Something wants a good look at us before it decides what to do about us,"* said Grimalkin, tail flicking with the particular stillness of a cat calculating rather than simply reacting. *"That's rarely a good sign, in my experience.*"

Gideon put himself, as he generally did, between the treeline and the camp, though this time without the theater — no headbutting a crate, simply standing, watching, waiting for a human to notice the shape of his attention and ask the right question.

It was Marian who noticed first, which surprised no one, though the *way* she noticed surprised Will considerably.

"Someone's watching the camp," she said, already on her feet, already moving toward Gideon's line of sight with a calm, unhurried certainty that had nothing panicked in it at all. "Has been for a few days, probably. We should have caught it sooner."

"How do you know that?" Will asked, genuinely curious rather than suspicious, though the question landed with a weight neither of them quite acknowledged.

"Gideon's been watching that treeline for the better part of an hour," Marian said, which was true, and answered nothing at all about how she'd known to trust a goat's attention span as reliable intelligence, and everyone let it pass regardless, the way people generally do with an answer that sounds complete enough not to invite the follow-up question.

---

**[PLATE 2 — the group investigating, finding evidence of a Sheriff's scout, tension building]**

They found the evidence within the hour — a cold fire pit, carefully hidden, positioned with a clear sightline down into the camp's lower approach, and boot prints belonging to a man who'd clearly been trained to move quietly and hadn't quite managed it around men who spent their days tracking game for supper.

"Sheriff's colors," Long John said, crouched over a scrap of fabric caught on a low branch, turning it over with two careful fingers. "Or near enough. Not a local hunter, not a highwayman. Someone sent, and sent deliberately."

"So it's started," Robin said, quietly.

"It was always going to start," Marian said, with a steadiness that was, if anyone had been paying especially close attention, rather more certain than the situation strictly justified. "We simply didn't know the shape of it yet. Now we do. That's not nothing."

*(new text page)*

"You're taking this remarkably well," Will observed, watching her fold the scrap of fabric away with brisk, methodical care, the same care she brought to everything that actually mattered.

"Would you prefer I panicked?"

"I'd prefer an explanation for how you're this calm about a stranger watching us sleep," Will said, not unkindly, though the question had real weight underneath the easy tone. "That's new, Marian. You've been different since — " He stopped himself, visibly, unable to name the exact evening he meant without sounding accusatory about something he didn't actually understand.

"I've had a great deal on my mind lately," Marian said, which was, of every answer available to her, the truest one she could offer without offering considerably more than she was permitted to.

---

**[PLATE 3 — Thistlewood arriving, noticing Marian's changed behavior toward him, a private moment of realization]**

Thistlewood arrived not long after, drawn by whatever means he was drawn by these days, and it was in the small unremarkable moment of his arrival — Marian crossing to update him on the scout's tracks with an ease and directness that bypassed every ounce of her usual careful watchfulness — that he stopped, mid-step, and looked at her properly for the first time that evening.

"You're not curious," he said, slowly, "how I already knew to come."

"I assumed you had your reasons," Marian said. "You generally do."

"Yes," Thistlewood agreed, watching her with an entirely new kind of attention now, "but you've never simply *assumed* that before. You've filed it. Turned it over. Looked at me the way a woman looks at a puzzle she hasn't been permitted to finish." He paused. "You're not looking at me that way now."

---

*(new text page)*

Marian held his gaze, steady, and made the only decision available to her that didn't involve an outright lie to a man who had, by every measure that mattered, earned better than that from her.

"Pip told me," she said, quietly, for his ears alone. "All of it. Some weeks ago now."

Something crossed Thistlewood's face — surprise first, unguarded and real, followed quickly by something more complicated that took him rather longer to settle into an expression he was willing to let her see.

"I see," he said, finally. "That was not, strictly, hers to give."

"No," Marian agreed. "But I'm glad she did. And I'd rather you were angry with me than with her, if anger's owed to anyone tonight."

---

**[PLATE 4 — Thistlewood's reaction settling: not anger, but a complicated acceptance, warmth underneath]**

Thistlewood was quiet long enough that Marian began, despite herself, to wonder if she'd misjudged the moment entirely. Then something in his posture eased, some old careful guardedness setting itself down the way a man sets down a weight he's grown so used to carrying he'd nearly forgotten it was a choice.

"I'm not angry," he said, eventually. "Startled. Rather more relieved than I expected to be, if I'm honest, which is its own kind of unsettling at my age." He allowed himself something almost like a smile. "It's a great deal, carrying a secret alone. I imagine Pip felt that as keenly as I do, most days. Perhaps more keenly, given how much longer she's had to manage without anyone to set part of it down with."

"I'm not going anywhere," Marian said. "Whatever you're both carrying — I can carry my share of it now too."

---

*(new text page)*

"Well," Thistlewood said, brisk again, the moment folding itself back into something more practical with the particular speed of a man who'd learned, over a very long life, exactly how much sentiment a single evening could reasonably hold. "Given that you already know precisely what I am, I don't suppose there's any further use pretending otherwise between the two of us. Shall we discuss what to actually do about a Sheriff's scout who's seen rather more of this camp than is comfortable for anyone?"

"Now that," Marian said, "sounds considerably more useful than either of us standing here being emotional about it."

"Quite," Thistlewood agreed, and if there was real warmth underneath the brisk tone, neither of them felt any particular need to remark on it further.

---

**[PLATE 5 — resolving the scout situation together, Marian and Thistlewood now openly collaborating in front of the others without explanation, the rest of the gang mildly baffled but going along with it]**

What followed was, by any reasonable measure, considerably more efficient than anything the group had managed on instinct alone — Thistlewood's read on the scout's likely position, filtered through Marian's own tactical sense, produced a plan sound enough that even Will's usual objections died somewhere around the second sentence.

"That's oddly specific," Robin said, watching the two of them work through the details with an ease that hadn't been there a week ago.

"Good specific, though," said Long John, already moving to position.

The scout, when they found him — young, nervous, clearly new to work considerably more dangerous than he'd been prepared for — surrendered with something close to relief once he understood the alternative, and offered, in exchange for safe passage home, rather more information about the Sheriff's mood than any of them had expected to get for free.

---

*(new text page)*

"He's not sending an army," the scout said, hands raised, doing his very best to be cooperative. "Not yet, anyway. He's sending *me*, and men like me, to learn what he's actually dealing with before he decides how big a hammer the problem needs. He's furious, but he's not stupid. Wanted to know your numbers. Your habits. Whether the stories about you were exaggerated."

"And what will you tell him?" Marian asked.

The scout considered this with the particular care of a young man realizing, mid-sentence, that his answer might matter rather more than he'd expected it to.

"That the stories weren't exaggerated nearly enough," he said, and left at a pace that suggested he meant every word of it.

Marian watched him go, then glanced once at Thistlewood — a small, private exchange that said considerably more than either of them would have put into words in front of the others — before turning back to the business of getting everyone home before full dark.
