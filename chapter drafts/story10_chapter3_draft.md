# Story 10: A Father's Reckoning
## Part Three: What Fathers Carry

*(Working title — happy to change.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — the group gathered at the fire, ready to hear the full story]**

They settled around the fire once evening properly arrived, the whole crew gathered in careful, attentive silence — not the easy warmth of an ordinary evening, but something more deliberate, more weighted, everyone present understanding, without needing to be told, exactly how much this particular conversation actually mattered.

"I owe most of you a proper introduction, before anything else," the man said, looking around at the gathered faces. "My name is Edmund. Edmund of Loxby, though the name carries considerably less weight these days than it once did." He glanced at Robin, something careful and hopeful in it. "I'll start at the beginning. Properly, this time. No more careful half-explanations."

"Please," Robin said, quiet, and found he meant it entirely.

"Years ago," Edmund began, "when our family's fortunes were rather better than they've been since, I made a mistake I've spent every year afterward regretting. The Sheriff — not so openly cruel then as he's since become, or perhaps simply better at concealing it — offered loans to families like ours, gentry with modest holdings and ambitions rather larger than our actual means. I took what he offered. I invested poorly, foolishly, in ventures that promised considerably more than they ever delivered. Within two years, I owed him more than our entire holding was worth."

"You never told me any of this," Robin said, something raw working beneath the careful control he was clearly trying to maintain.

"I was ashamed," Edmund said, simply. "A father's failures are rather harder to confess to his own son than to anyone else alive."

---

**[PLATE 2 — the danger escalating, Edmund's fear for Robin's safety]**

"The debt alone I might have managed, eventually, through patience and hard work," Edmund continued. "What frightened me considerably more was watching how the Sheriff began looking at you, once you were old enough that your own prospects became relevant to his calculations. A debt like mine doesn't simply disappear. It becomes leverage, eventually, in whatever hands are patient enough to wait for the right moment to use it. I became convinced, rightly or wrongly, that he intended to use you against me one day — as a hostage to guarantee my continued compliance, or worse."

"So you tried to send me away," Robin said, understanding dawning slow and painful across his features.

"I tried to make you safe," Edmund said. "I arranged a marriage — a good family, genuinely protective connections, a position considerably beyond our own fallen means to secure any other way. I thought, if I could simply move you beyond the Sheriff's easy reach, the danger I'd created would stay contained to me alone, where it properly belonged."

---

*(new text page)*

"You never explained any of that," Robin said, the old hurt surfacing plainly now, no longer buried beneath politeness. "You simply announced it. A wife I'd never met, a life I'd never chosen, presented to me as though my own preferences were entirely beside the point."

"I know," Edmund said, and there was real, unguarded regret in it. "I told myself, at the time, that explaining the danger would only frighten you without actually protecting you any better. I told myself a great many convenient things, if I'm honest about it now. The truth is simpler, and considerably less flattering: I was ashamed, and afraid, and I chose control over honesty because honesty required admitting failures I wasn't yet brave enough to properly confess."

---

**[PLATE 3 — Robin's reaction: the reframing, complicated emotions surfacing]**

"I refused the marriage," Robin said, quiet, working through the shape of his own memory with new, considerably more painful clarity. "We fought. You said I was throwing away every advantage you'd tried to secure me. I said you were treating me like property to be traded rather than a son to be loved. I left that same week, and I never—" He stopped, something catching in his throat that he made no particular effort to hide. "I never properly understood, until this exact moment, that you weren't trying to control my life out of simple pride. You were trying to save it."

"Both things were likely true at once," Edmund said, honest even now. "I won't pretend my pride played no part in how badly I handled it. But yes — beneath the pride, beneath the clumsy, controlling way I went about it, I was trying to save you. I've simply never known how to properly say that, until a debt considerably larger than money finally forced the words out of me."

---

*(new text page)*

Thistlewood, who had said nothing through the whole account, finally spoke, his own weathered voice carrying quiet confirmation of everything Edmund had described. "That part, at least, I can confirm from my own memory of it," he said, gently. "A gambling debt, foolishly large, forgiven rather than collected — Edmund's kindness, back when either of us could still afford such generosity toward the other. When word reached me that his son had gone, and gone badly, I called in that old favor myself, before Edmund even properly asked it of me. I simply told him I intended to look after you, Robin, whatever that required, and he trusted me enough not to ask for the particulars."

"You never told me any of this either," Robin said, looking between the two old friends with something like wondering disbelief. "All this time, and neither of you thought to simply say it plainly."

"Some truths take longer to feel safe telling than others," Edmund said, quiet. "I never told Thistlewood the full shape of my own failures, even once he'd taken it upon himself to guard the son they'd cost me. He never once asked me for the particulars. I simply let him manage the safety however his own considerable skill saw fit, grateful beyond words that I no longer had to carry that particular burden entirely alone."

Pip, sitting close beside Thistlewood, said nothing, though her expression carried real, quiet sympathy for exactly how much weight both old men had apparently spent years carrying in careful, separate silence.

---

**[PLATE 4 — closing: the truth fully told, real but imperfect processing beginning]**

The fire burned low as the full weight of it finally settled over the gathered group, nobody quite ready to speak into the particular heavy quiet that followed a truth this large finally, completely, spoken aloud.

"That's rather a lot to carry alone," Long John said, finally, gentle as ever, breaking the silence with the particular simple kindness that had become, by now, exactly what the moment needed.

"It is," Edmund agreed. "I've carried it considerably longer than I should have, at Robin's expense as much as my own. I don't expect forgiveness tonight. I don't know that I've properly earned it, even now, having finally explained myself. I simply hope, having told the whole truth at last, that we might begin finding our way back toward each other, however long that particular journey actually takes."

---

*(new text page)*

Robin was quiet a long while, turning the whole shape of his own history over with the particular careful attention he'd learned, across a difficult year, to give anything that actually mattered.

"I don't forgive you," he said, finally, honest rather than cruel, "not entirely, not yet. Too many years of hurt don't simply dissolve because the reasons behind them finally make sense." He looked at his father directly, something tentative and genuinely hopeful working beneath the honest admission. "But I understand you now, in a way I never properly did before. That's not nothing. I find I'd like to see where that understanding actually leads us, given time and rather more honesty than either of us has managed until tonight."

"That's more than I hoped for," Edmund said, quiet, genuine gratitude plain in his weathered features. "I'll take it gladly, and however much more you're willing to offer me, whenever you're properly ready to offer it."
