# Story: Six Degrees of Sherwood
## Part One: Robin of Loxby

*(Working titles — happy to change either.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — opening image: Robin practicing archery in a Sherwood clearing, badly]**

Robin of Loxby had been practicing his aim for the better part of two years, which is to say he had been *missing* things for the better part of two years, with a dedication that in any other pursuit would have been called stubbornness and in this one was generously called practice. The target — a sack of straw wearing, for reasons no one now remembered, an old boot on top — had survived every single one of his arrows so far, and had begun, in the manner of things that survive long enough, to develop something like an attitude about it.

This was not, strictly speaking, Robin's fault. Loxby men had always been fine archers, going back generations, in the same way that Loxby men had always been minor gentry, and mildly disappointing, and bad at cards. Robin had inherited two of these three things reliably. The third he was still working on, mostly by missing at it very hard, very often, in a clearing three miles from anywhere in particular.

He also had, tucked under a loose floorboard in the cottage he refused to call a family home, one unopened letter, several months old, sealed with his father's mark. He had told himself six different reasons why he hadn't opened it. None of them were true. The real reason was considerably smaller and more embarrassing than any of the six, which is usually how these things go, and he was not going to think about it today, on account of already having a full schedule of missing things.

He nocked another arrow. He missed. Somewhere, a boot on a sack of straw continued its long and undefeated career.

---

**[PLATE 2 — the tax collector's seizure in a village square: cart being loaded with a family's goods, a woman pleading, guards standing by]**

It was on his way back from not hitting anything that he came across the business in the village — not his village, precisely, though he'd stopped thinking of the distinction as meaningful some time ago. A cart stood in the square, half-loaded with sacks of grain, a small unhappy flock of geese, and, being carried out at that exact moment, a stool that had plainly been someone's grandmother's and was not going to be anyone's grandmother's again if the man carrying it had anything to say about it.

The man doing the carrying wore the King's colors the way a bully wears a smile — technically correctly, and to no one's comfort. Behind him stood two guards with the particular posture of men who have been paid to look imposing and are largely succeeding. In front of him stood a woman who was doing the actual work of the scene, which was being furious while trying not to get anyone hurt, a skill Robin had never had cause to learn and rather wished, watching her, that he had.

"Twice this year," she was saying. "You've had the tax twice this year already."

"Then you'll want to be more careful about owing it a third time," said the tax man, in the tone of someone who has said this line before and enjoyed it each time.

Robin did what Robin generally did in these situations, which was to have a fully formed plan in roughly four seconds and no second plan whatsoever.

---

**[PLATE 3 — Robin stepping out with his bow, dramatically posed but clearly about to go wrong]**

The plan, insofar as it existed, was this: step out into the square, draw his bow, say something commanding about the King's justice and a man's own hearth, and rely on the general terror of an armed stranger to do the rest. It was, Robin felt, a good plan. It had only one flaw, which was the part where he tried to execute it.

He stepped out. He drew. He opened his mouth to say something commanding about the King's justice.

What came out instead was a fairly normal-volume "Excuse me—" followed immediately by the arrow leaving the bow roughly forty degrees from where he had intended it to go, on account of his boot choosing that precise moment to find a loose cobble.

The arrow did not hit the tax man. It did not hit either guard. It flew, with a gentle indifference to all human planning, directly into the rope holding the cart's tailgate shut, which parted with a small, almost apologetic *twang*.

---

**[PLATE 4 — chaos: the cart's contents spilling out, geese loose and honking, guards scrambling, the tax man furious, Robin frozen mid-reaction]**

What followed was not, by any reasonable definition, a rescue. It was closer to an act of God with excellent comic timing. The tailgate swung open. The sacks of grain slid out. The geese, sensing an opportunity that geese are unusually good at sensing, launched themselves at the nearest guard with the enthusiasm of creatures who have been sitting in a cart for an hour and have opinions about it.

In the resulting few seconds — during which one guard discovered he was considerably more afraid of geese than of outlaws, the tax man attempted to catch a rolling sack of grain and instead caught the ground, and the grandmother's stool was quietly reclaimed by a boy of about nine who had clearly been waiting his whole short life for this exact opportunity — the family's goods ended up, more or less, back where they'd started. Nobody could have planned it. Robin certainly hadn't. He stood in the middle of the square holding a bow, entirely unshot-at, wearing the expression of a man watching a miracle he cannot take credit for and is not currently in a position to explain.

"Bell and book!" someone shouted, pointing at Robin — and Robin, still catching up with events, could only frown, because he had genuinely no idea what that meant, and this was, if he'd stopped to count, the eleventh time this year.

---

**[PLATE 5 — Long John Little entering the chaos: enormous, clearly a hired guard/enforcer, panicking, about to make things much worse or much better]**

It was at this point that the tax man's third guard arrived — a man so enormous that his arrival alone knocked a display of pottery from a nearby stall, an event he apologized to immediately and with real feeling. He had, up to this point, been around the corner dealing with a separate errand, and he returned now to find his employer on the ground, his fellow guard being personally addressed by several geese, and a stranger standing in the middle of it all holding a bow he clearly hadn't hit anything with.

"Right," said the enormous man, with the deep unhappiness of someone about to do a job he had never enjoyed even slightly. "You'll — I'm going to need you to come with me, I'm afraid. Very sorry."

He took one step forward. The step landed on a sack of spilled grain, which slid, which took his foot with it, which took the rest of him with it, directly into the remaining half of the cart, which came apart under him with the tired finality of furniture that has simply had enough.

"Terribly sorry," he said, to the cart, from inside it.

Robin, given an opening no plan of his had ever produced, took it.

---

**[PLATE 6 — Robin and Long John fleeing together toward the treeline, breathless, an odd pair already]**

They ran. It was not a dignified run — the enormous man, once extracted from the cart, moved like a landslide with second thoughts, and Robin kept glancing back to make sure the family had actually gotten their stool — but it got them both to the treeline, and then some considerable way past it, before either of them stopped to consider what exactly they'd just done.

"I ought to mention," said the enormous man, once he'd got his breath back, "that I don't actually know why I'm running. You were the one they wanted."

"I didn't especially want to be caught," Robin admitted.

"No. Fair. Nor me, now I think on it." He extended an enormous hand. "Little John. Well — Long John. People generally settle on the one that isn't true and the one that's an insult, and somehow I've ended up with both."

"Robin. Of Loxby."

"Pleasure," said Long John, in the tone of a man reassessing his entire afternoon and finding, to his mild surprise, that he didn't much mind how it had gone.

They walked on a little further before Robin, who had been turning it over, finally asked. "Someone called me a 'bell and book' back there. Is that— is that a village near here? I didn't think we'd gone far enough to have crossed into anywhere new."

Long John looked at him for a long moment, the particular look of a large gentle man recalibrating his expectations of a new acquaintance downward, gently, so as not to hurt anyone's feelings.

"It means *crook*," he said. "Thief. On account of 'book' and 'crook' — well. It rhymes."

"Then why not simply say thief?"

"I couldn't tell you, Robin. I genuinely could not tell you." He said it kindly. He clearly meant it kindly. It was, all the same, the first of what would turn out to be a very great many times he would have to say something very similar.

Somewhere behind them, a goose was still being extremely rude to a guard, a boy had gone home with his grandmother's stool, and a tax man sat in the dust of the square doing furious arithmetic that would not, this month, come out in his favor.

Neither Robin nor Long John looked back to see any of it. They had, by unspoken and immediate agreement, decided that whatever came next, it would probably go better with two of them than with one — which, as first impressions of a friendship go, was not a bad one at all.
