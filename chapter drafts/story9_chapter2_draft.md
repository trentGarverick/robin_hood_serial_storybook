# Story 9: The Whole Truth
## Part Two: One Family, Fully

**[PLATE 1 — the group gathering, anticipation building, Will/Long John/Crumb sensing something significant is coming]**

They gathered everyone at the cottage that evening, and it took no more than a glance around the room for Will to understand, with real and immediate certainty, that whatever this was, it was considerably more significant than an ordinary supper invitation.

"You've all got the look of people about to tell us something," he said, settling onto a bench with careful, deliberate casualness. "The particular look. I've seen it enough times now to recognize it properly."

"You're not wrong," Marian said. "Robin's asked us to tell you something. All three of you. Something we've been keeping from you for rather longer than any of us are entirely proud of."

Long John, ever steady, simply nodded and waited, patient as always. Brother Crumb said nothing at all, watching the gathered faces with the particular quiet attentiveness of a man who had spent considerably longer noticing things than anyone generally gave him credit for.

"There's no easy way to simply say this," Robin began, "so I'll say it plainly, and let the proof speak for whatever words fail to properly convey." He looked to Thistlewood. "Go ahead."

Thistlewood rose, staff in hand, and something in his bearing shifted — the mild, fussy scholar falling away, revealing, gently this time, without crisis or danger forcing the moment, the same older, weightier presence Robin had first witnessed in Crane's confrontation.

"I am a wizard," he said, plainly, warmly, watching their faces with real care for how the words might land. "Have been for rather longer than any of you have known me. It's why the luck around this camp has always run so improbably in your favor. It's rather past time you all understood exactly why."

---

**[PLATE 2 — the demonstration: calm, controlled, warm rather than desperate; Pip's gender reveal in the same moment]**

Light gathered at the staff's tip, soft and warm this time, no bent air or frightening intensity to it — simply small, drifting motes of gold light that rose gently toward the rafters like a scattering of fireflies, settling over the astonished faces below with a gentleness that felt, deliberately, like reassurance rather than proof.

"Well," Will managed, after a long moment of simply staring. "That's rather more than I was expecting from supper."

Pip stepped forward then, steady, no trace of her old practiced boyishness left in her bearing at all. "There's more," she said. "I'm not his grandson. Never have been. My name's still Pip — that much was always true. But everything else about how I've presented myself to all of you has been exactly as false as everything Thistlewood's just shown you was real."

The silence that followed held real weight, three faces working through disbelief in three entirely distinct ways, before Will, predictably, was the first to properly break it.

"I've been out-fought by a *girl* this whole time," he said, though there was no real complaint in it, only the beginnings of something that might, given a moment longer, become genuine delight. "I don't know whether to be embarrassed or impressed. Possibly both. I generally find I can manage both at once, these days."

"You generally can," Marian agreed, dry, watching him with real warmth beneath the familiar teasing.

---

**[PLATE 3 — each character's distinct reaction: Will's shock softening into loyalty, Long John's steady acceptance]**

"I know a thing or two, myself, about carrying something too long that costs more to keep hidden than it would ever cost to finally set down," Will said, quieter now, something genuinely serious moving beneath the easy humor. "If any of you were worried how I'd take this — don't be. Whatever else I am, I'm not a man who abandons people for the shape of truths they've been protecting rather than the harm they've actually done."

"Nobody doubted you, Will," Robin said, warm.

"Good," Will said, recovering some of his usual brightness. "Because I intend to have a great many questions later, and I'd rather ask them as a trusted member of this conspiracy than as someone still being carefully managed."

---

*(new text page)*

Long John, who had said almost nothing through the whole revelation, finally spoke, his voice carrying its usual steady, unhurried warmth. "Well," he said, simply. "That explains rather a lot, actually. The staff. The way Pip's always fought like someone who'd trained a good deal harder than her apparent years should allow." He looked between them all, something plain and unshakeable in his expression. "Doesn't change anything that actually matters to me. You're still the same people I've been glad to call family this past year. That's rather the only part of this I needed properly settled."

"That's it?" Will asked, faintly incredulous. "No shock? No demanding a second demonstration?"

"I believe what I've seen with my own eyes," Long John said, simply. "Seemed rather thorough enough the first time."

---

**[PLATE 4 — Crumb's reaction: unbothered matter-of-factness, revealing he'd half-suspected something]**

It was Brother Crumb, last to speak, who offered the response none of them had quite properly anticipated.

"I did wonder," he said, mild, entirely unbothered, reaching for his usual mug of something warm as though the evening's revelations were simply one more ordinary occurrence to be quietly absorbed alongside everything else. "Fifteen years at the Abbey teaches a man to read people rather more carefully than they generally realize they're being read. An old man who turns up at exactly the right moment, capable in ways he never quite explains — I'd noticed the shape of it, if not the particulars. I simply decided, rather early on, that it wasn't my business to go prying into secrets people clearly needed kept."

"You *suspected*?" Robin asked, startled. "This whole time?"

---

*(new text page)*

"Suspected is rather a strong word," Crumb said, thoughtfully. "I noticed a shape that didn't quite add up, and decided the people I trusted deserved my patience rather than my curiosity. I find that's generally served me rather better than asking too many questions too early ever has." He looked around at the gathered group, something warm and entirely sincere beneath his usual placid calm. "I'm glad you've decided to tell us properly, all the same. A shape you've noticed is a poor substitute for a truth you've actually been given."

"Fifteen years of noticing things," Will said, shaking his head, "and you never once said anything."

"I'm a friar, Will," Crumb said, mildly. "Discretion is rather the whole of the job description, most days."

The whole room, despite everything, found itself laughing together — the particular, easy laughter of a family finally, properly, entirely whole.
