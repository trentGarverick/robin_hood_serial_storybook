# Story: Six Degrees of Sherwood
## Part Seven: An Unusual Household

*(Working title — happy to change. Second of three closing parts.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — the group arriving at Thistlewood's cluttered cottage, Robin being helped inside, first impressions of the strange, book-filled home]**

The old man's home turned out to be some distance further than any of them had the strength to properly complain about, though Will managed a respectable effort regardless. It was a cottage that seemed, from the outside, entirely too small to contain what it turned out to contain — shelves stacked two deep with books, herbs drying in bundles from every beam, and a clutter that somehow read as careful rather than chaotic, the specific disorder of a man who knew exactly where everything was and would be mildly offended if anyone tidied it.

"Sit him there," the old man instructed, and Long John, gentle as ever despite his size, settled Robin onto a low couch that had clearly not been designed with a man Robin's height in mind.

"You have a great many books," Marian observed, looking around with the frank, cataloguing attention of a woman who noticed everything and forgot nothing.

"I have a great many opinions," the old man said, "and not enough people willing to argue them with me. The books manage adequately as a substitute."

"You still haven't told us your name," Will pointed out, settling into a chair with the easy confidence of a man who had decided, some time ago, that hospitality was owed to him rather than the reverse.

The old man busied himself with a kettle for a moment longer than the task strictly required.

"Thistlewood," he said eventually. "Master Thistlewood, if we're being formal about it, which I generally find we needn't be." He nodded toward the boy, who had settled cross-legged near the fire with the loose-limbed ease of someone entirely at home. "Pip."

"Just Pip?" Robin asked, from the couch, wincing as he shifted.

"Just Pip," Pip agreed, in a voice that offered nothing further and clearly had no intention of doing so.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Robin resting while Thistlewood tends the wound, a quiet moment between them]**

Thistlewood tended the wound with the same brisk, unbothered competence he'd shown on the hillside, unwrapping and re-dressing it with hands that moved with more certainty than an old man's hands had any obvious right to.

"You'll live," he said, not for the first time. "Rest, and don't be a fool about it, and you'll live comfortably. Fail to rest, and you'll live regardless, but you'll spend a great deal longer complaining about it, and so will everyone around you."

"You keep saying that like you've watched a great many young men ignore good advice," Robin said.

Something crossed Thistlewood's face then — brief, unreadable, gone before Robin could properly examine it. "I have," he said simply, and did not elaborate, and busied himself instead with tucking the blanket more firmly than strictly necessary around Robin's shoulders.

"You're very kind, for a stranger," Robin added, half into the pillow, already halfway to sleep in the particular defenseless way of the genuinely exhausted.

"I'm not especially kind," Thistlewood said, quietly, mostly to himself, once Robin's breathing had evened out into something like real rest. "I simply have an old debt I'm rather more invested in than I once expected to be."

If Pip, still sitting by the fire, heard this, he gave no visible sign of it — though the corner of his mouth did something that might, in a less careful light, have looked almost like a smile he was trying not to have.

---

**[PLATE 3 — the animals introduced: Gideon, Grimalkin, and Barley, each with a distinct personality moment, the crew reacting]**

It was Will who found the goat, mostly because the goat found him first, in the specific sense of eating four inches off the end of his second-favorite sleeve before he'd so much as sat down in the yard.

"That is an *animal*," Will said, with real betrayal, snatching the remainder of the sleeve back.

"That's Gideon," said Pip, unhelpfully, not looking up from whatever he was doing to the fire. "He's very reasonable, generally."

"He is *eating my clothing.*"

"Yes," Pip agreed. "That's the unreasonable part. No one's ever caught him at it directly, mind you. He's terribly good about that."

The cat arrived shortly after, with the unhurried confidence of a creature who had never once in its life needed to introduce itself to anybody. It surveyed Long John with open, undisguised skepticism, gave Will a single dismissive glance that somehow communicated an entire opinion, and made its way, with sudden and complete purpose, directly to Marian, where it installed itself in her lap without so much as a by-your-leave.

"Grimalkin doesn't do that," said Pip, with what sounded almost like surprise. "He doesn't do that for *anyone*."

Marian looked down at the cat, who had already settled into the deeply satisfied posture of an animal that had made a decision and did not intend to discuss it further, and said nothing — though her hand, after a moment, found its way to scratching behind his ears anyway, and something in her expression suggested she was filing this away with all the other small, unexplainable things.

---

**[PLATE 4 — Barley bursting in, chaotic and food-motivated, chaos ensuing]**

The dog announced itself before it arrived, which was generally how the dog did most things. Barley came around the side of the cottage at a dead sprint, ears flying, entirely undone by the mere existence of new people to greet, and managed to knock over both a stack of firewood and Brother Crumb's carefully guarded lunch in the same enthusiastic pass.

"*No,*" said Brother Crumb, with real anguish, lunging for the bread half a second too late.

Barley, entirely unrepentant, finished the bread, sat, and looked up at Crumb with an expression of such profound, uncomplicated devotion that Crumb's outrage visibly struggled against it and, after a moment, lost.

"You are," Crumb said, scratching the dog's ears despite himself, "a beggar's-cloak of a thief, you know that?"

Robin, half-asleep, roused just enough. "A what?"

"*Joke*, Robin," said the entire room, in the weary unison that had, by now, stopped even pretending to be surprising.

Later, once the humans had drifted off to their various corners of the cottage and yard — Robin asleep properly now, the others scattered in the particular loose contentment of people who'd survived something and were still absorbing it — the three animals convened, as they generally did, in the low branches of the yard's one crooked apple tree.

"Well," said Grimalkin, settling with his customary disdain into the fork of the branch. "They seem exactly as much trouble as advertised."

"I like them," said Barley, from the ground below, tail still going. "The big one gives good scratches."

"You like everyone who has hands," said Gideon, not unkindly, chewing thoughtfully on something that had, until recently, belonged to somebody's laundry line. "It isn't a rigorous standard."

"It's a perfectly good standard," said Barley, wounded.

---

**[PLATE 5 — Marian sitting alone with Grimalkin, a quiet character beat, her suspicion visibly growing but unspoken]**

Marian found a quiet corner of the yard as evening settled, Grimalkin draped over her shoulders with the proprietary comfort of a cat who had apparently decided this arrangement was now permanent, and allowed herself, for the first time in what felt like a very long week, to simply sit with her own thoughts.

They were not entirely comfortable thoughts. An old man who fought like no old man she'd ever seen. A boy who moved like he'd trained for it his whole short life. A cat who had chosen her, instantly and completely, the way cats generally didn't choose anyone. None of it, taken alone, meant anything at all. Taken together, it had the shape of a puzzle she hadn't been given all the pieces to — and Marian had never much cared for puzzles she wasn't allowed to finish.

"You know something," she said, quietly, to the cat, who offered no response beyond a slow, unbothered blink. "I don't suppose you'd tell me, even if you could."

Grimalkin's tail flicked once, which she chose, for lack of any better information, to interpret as *no.*

---

**[PLATE 6 — the crew preparing to leave, warm farewells, Thistlewood and Pip watching them go]**

They stayed three days in the end — long enough for Robin's wound to close properly, long enough for Will's sleeve to be, against all reasonable expectation, mended by Pip with a skill that bordered on suspicious and was, by unspoken agreement, not questioned too closely. When it came time to leave, the goodbyes had the particular awkwardness of people who had shared something real and didn't quite have the practice to say so plainly.

"You'll look after them," Thistlewood said to Marian, apropos of nothing, as the others gathered their things.

"That's rather been the job," Marian said, "since before I met you."

"Yes," Thistlewood agreed, with something that might, in anyone else, have been called warmth. "I rather thought it might have been."

"Come back through, if the road brings you this way again," Pip added, with a studied casualness that didn't entirely disguise how much he meant it. "Gideon will have opinions about it either way, so you may as well make it worth his while."

"We heard that," came a flat voice from somewhere near the vegetable patch, followed by the distinct sound of something else being eaten that almost certainly shouldn't have been.

Robin, on his feet properly now for the first time in three days, extended a hand to Thistlewood, who took it with a grip steadier than any of them expected from a man his apparent age.

"Thank you," Robin said, simply. "For the wound. For three days under your roof, and the meals, and putting up with the rest of us besides. We'd have managed a great deal worse without you."

"You're very welcome," said Thistlewood, and if his voice caught, just slightly, on the last word, no one standing there was close enough, yet, to notice.
