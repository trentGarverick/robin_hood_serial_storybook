# Story 10: A Father's Reckoning
## Part Four: Small Steps

**[PLATE 1 — Edmund watching Robin practice archery, a bittersweet, warm moment]**

Edmund stayed, in the days that followed, in the particular careful way of a man testing whether his welcome was genuine or simply extended out of obligation — helping where help was wanted, keeping quietly to himself where it wasn't, watching his son's new life unfold around him with an attention that carried more wonder in it than he seemed entirely prepared for.

It was watching Robin practice archery, on the second morning, that finally cracked something loose in his careful composure.

"You've been at this how long?" Edmund asked, watching an arrow sail wide of its target by a margin that seemed, to his eye, almost impressively consistent.

"The better part of two years," Robin admitted, retrieving the arrow with the long-suffering patience of a man who had made peace with his own limitations. "I'm told the improbable success rate makes up for the poor aim. I've stopped questioning it too closely."

Edmund laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, the first Robin had heard from his father since arriving. "Your grandfather was the same," he said, something warm working through old grief. "Worst shot in the county, and somehow never once seriously injured by his own poor aim. I'd nearly forgotten that, until just now."

"I don't remember him," Robin said, quiet.

"No," Edmund said. "You were young, when he passed. I should have told you more stories about him, over the years. I should have told you a great many things, if I'm honest about the whole shape of what I failed to offer you." He watched Robin nock another arrow, something openly proud beneath the old regret. "You've found something rather remarkable here, regardless of how poorly I managed my own part in getting you to it."

**[PLATE 2 — Edmund meeting Marian properly, a meaningful, warm-but-careful scene]**

Marian found him alone by the stream that afternoon, and approached with the particular deliberate directness she brought to anything she considered genuinely important.

"We've not been properly introduced," she said, extending a hand. "Marian Fletcher."

"Edmund," he said, taking it, studying her with open, unhurried attention. "Robin's spoken of you. Rather more, I think, than he realizes he has."

"Has he," Marian said, dry, though something in her expression carried real warmth beneath the deflection.

"I arranged a marriage for him once," Edmund said, quiet, watching the stream rather than her face, something careful in how he chose the admission. "A good family, considerable protection, a transaction dressed up as a kindness. I understand now precisely how poorly that reflects on my own understanding of what actually matters between two people." He looked at her directly, finally. "Whatever this is, between you and my son — it wasn't arranged, was it. It wasn't a transaction."

"No," Marian said, steady. "It was rather the opposite of arranged, if I'm honest. It took the better part of a year, considerable stubbornness on both our parts, and neither of us particularly planning for it to happen at all."

"Good," Edmund said, something genuinely moved beneath the simple word. "That's precisely how it ought to happen. I'm glad he found his way to something real, whatever my own poor example failed to teach him about the difference."

---

**[PLATE 3 — broader integration with the found family, warmth and humor]**

By evening, Edmund had been thoroughly absorbed into the camp's usual chaos — Brother Crumb pressing a third helping on him with the particular insistent generosity that brooked no argument, Will attempting to explain the cant gag with visible relish while Edmund grew increasingly, delightedly confused by the whole enterprise, Long John simply offering him a place by the fire with the same unhurried warmth he extended to everyone.

"Your son," Crumb said, settling beside him, "cheats rather terribly at cards, in case nobody's mentioned it."

"That does track with everything else I've learned about him today," Edmund said, and found himself laughing again, easier this time, the particular unguarded laugh of a man who hadn't realized how much he'd missed simply being included somewhere.

Thistlewood, watching the whole scene with quiet satisfaction, finally settled beside his old friend. "You've found yourself rather a family here, alongside your son," he observed.

"I hadn't expected that," Edmund admitted. "I came prepared for a difficult conversation and a dangerous warning. I hadn't properly prepared myself for how much I'd want to stay, once the difficult part was actually behind us."

"Few of us properly prepare for that part," Thistlewood said, dry. "It rather sneaks up on you, this particular kind of belonging. I've found that's generally how the best of it works."

---

**[PLATE 4 — the warning arrives: Blackwood's forces mobilizing, urgency creeping under the warmth]**

It was Wat, arriving at a dead run just as the evening's warmth had properly settled, who broke the moment with news that none of them had quite let themselves forget was coming.

"Blackwood's moving," he reported, breathless. "Proper column this time — more soldiers than anyone's seen her bring before. They're not hiding it, either. Word's spreading through every village between here and Nottingham."

The warmth of the evening didn't vanish entirely, but something in it sobered all at once, every face around the fire recalculating the shape of exactly how much time their small, hard-won reconciliation had actually been given to properly settle.

"How long?" Marian asked, already moving toward practicality.

"Two days," Wat said. "Perhaps three, if the weather holds against her."

Edmund's expression, watching the gathered group brace themselves with the particular grim efficiency of people who'd done this before, carried something fierce and entirely unexpected beneath the fear. "Then we have two days," he said, "to prepare properly for whatever's coming. I've cost this family enough already, simply by being found. I don't intend to sit idle while the rest of you face the consequences of my own old debt."

"You're not simply sitting idle," Robin said, quiet, meeting his father's eyes with something that hadn't been there a few days prior — real trust, however new, however carefully earned. "You're family now, however that happened. Family doesn't sit idle when the rest of us are bracing for a fight."
