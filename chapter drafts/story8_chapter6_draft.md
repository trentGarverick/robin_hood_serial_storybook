# Story 8: The Witchfinder's Patience
## Part Six: The Truth, At Last

*(Working title — happy to change. Final chapter of this 6-part story — MAJOR permanent developments. Please read very carefully.)*

---

**[PLATE 1 — Crane returns with a final ultimatum, real urgency and danger]**

Crane returned on the fourth day, and this time he did not come alone — two silent men accompanied him, neither introduced, both carrying themselves with the particular official stillness of men present to enforce rather than simply observe.

"Master Thistlewood," Crane said, and something in his voice had shifted since the last visit, patience finally giving way to something harder underneath it. "I've written my findings. Sent them ahead, to be delivered should I not return by tomorrow's evening with a satisfactory explanation. A button. A staff. A grandson who fights too well and ages, by more than one witness's account, rather less than he ought to. I've run out of patience for coincidence."

"And if I've no explanation to satisfy you?" Thistlewood asked, quiet, already understanding exactly what answer was coming.

"Then my findings reach the Sheriff regardless," Crane said. "And considerably worse men than me take up the questioning from there."

Robin, standing beside Marian in the gathered doorway, felt something cold settle through him at the flat certainty in Crane's voice — not cruelty, exactly, simply the immovable conviction of a man who had finally run out of room for doubt.

"There's nowhere left to run this," Marian breathed, low, to Thistlewood alone. "Not with men already standing at your door."

Thistlewood was quiet a long moment, something old and heavy settling across his features — the particular stillness of a man arriving, at last, at a decision he'd spent considerably longer avoiding than anyone watching had properly realized.

"No," he said, finally. "I don't believe there is."

He turned to face Crane fully, and something in his bearing changed entirely — the mild, unremarkable scholar falling away all at once, revealing, for the first time in Robin's presence, someone considerably older and considerably more than he had ever once appeared to be.

---

**[PLATE 2 — Thistlewood reveals the truth openly, real undeniable magic performed]**

"You want proof," Thistlewood said, and his voice carried a weight it had never once held before, something ancient and certain beneath the familiar fussy cadence. "Proof you shall have, Witchfinder, though I suspect it will cost considerably more than either of us properly wants it to."

He raised his staff, and the air around it seemed, impossibly, to bend — light gathering and twisting at its tip, gathering into something that defied every ordinary explanation any of Crane's men might have reached for. One of the silent guards took an instinctive step backward. Crane himself did not move at all, though his composure, for the first time in five visits, finally, visibly cracked.

"You're—" he started, and could not, for a long moment, find the word he needed.

"A wizard," Thistlewood said, plainly. "Yes. Precisely what you've suspected, for rather longer than either of us has enjoyed admitting."

---

*(new text page)*

Robin, watching from the doorway, felt the whole shape of the last several months finally, permanently rearrange itself around a truth he had spent so long circling without ever quite letting himself land on it directly. The staff. The horses. Every improbable, impossible piece of luck that had carried him through a year he'd never properly earned the survival of on his own merits alone.

Beside him, Pip stepped forward — no longer performing the careful, practiced boyishness she'd worn for so long, but standing plainly, openly, exactly as she actually was.

"And I'm not his grandson," she said, steady, meeting Robin's eyes directly for the first time without a single trace of the old performance behind it. "I never have been. My name is still Pip. Everything else about how I've presented myself to you has been exactly as false as everything Thistlewood's just shown you was true."

Robin looked between them — the wizard, and the woman who had fought beside them for a year disguised as a boy — and found, to his own considerable surprise, that the truth landed not as betrayal, but as something closer to relief.

---

**[PLATE 3 — Crane's reaction, the confrontation reaching its peak, the group bracing for what comes next]**

Crane stared at the impossible light still gathered at the staff's tip, something in his expression working through disbelief, vindication, and something considerably more frightened than either, all at once.

"I was right," he said, quietly, almost to himself. "All this time. I was *right.*"

"You were," Thistlewood agreed. "And now you understand precisely why I've spent so long trying to ensure you never would be able to prove it." He took a slow step forward, staff still raised, expression carrying more genuine regret than threat. "I take no pleasure in what comes next, Witchfinder. I want you to understand that plainly, before it happens. You are not a wicked man. You have simply been considerably too good at your work for anyone's comfort, mine especially."

"What are you—" Crane started, and the two silent guards behind him shifted, uncertain, hands moving toward weapons they suddenly seemed considerably less confident would help them.

"Stand down," Marian said, sharp, stepping forward with her bow already raised, steady and unhesitating. "Nobody here means your men harm, provided they don't force the matter. This ends without bloodshed, if everyone remaining calm allows it to."

The moment held, tense and fragile, before Crane, some final calculation working behind his eyes, raised a hand to still his own guards.

"Do what you intend to do," he said, to Thistlewood, with the particular flat resignation of a man who understood, finally and completely, that he had walked into a confrontation he was never actually equipped to win. "I've spent my whole career chasing the truth. I find I've little appetite left for running from it, now that it's finally standing in front of me."

Thistlewood's expression, at that, carried something close to genuine sorrow.

---

**[PLATE 4 — the memory-alteration: Thistlewood's costly, rare ability, visible strain and toll]**

"This will not hurt you," Thistlewood said, quiet, raising the staff once more, though this time the light that gathered at its tip carried a different quality entirely — softer, more careful, considerably more precise than the raw display moments before. "I promise you that much, whatever else I take from you today."

"What are you doing to me?" Crane asked, and for the first time since any of them had met him, real fear broke plainly through his careful composure.

"Softening," Thistlewood said. "Not erasing — I find I haven't the stomach for taking a man's memory whole, whatever else I've become tonight. Simply... clouding. What you've seen will feel, by tomorrow, like something closer to a dream than a memory. Real enough to trouble you. Uncertain enough that no court, no Sheriff, no Church authority will ever accept it as testimony."

The working of it cost him visibly — his hands trembling by the time the light finally faded, his breathing labored in a way none of them had ever witnessed from him before, decades of careful restraint clearly straining against whatever this particular magic actually demanded of the person performing it.

---

*(new text page)*

Crane swayed slightly where he stood, blinking hard, something already softening and uncertain behind his eyes.

"I remember," he said, slowly, testing the words like a man uncertain of his own footing, "a light. Something at the end of a staff. I remember thinking I understood something important, and I cannot, for the life of me, presently recall precisely what that understanding actually was." He looked at Thistlewood with real, genuine confusion, the sharp, methodical certainty that had defined him across five visits entirely, visibly, gone. "Did something happen here? I feel as though something happened here."

"You asked a great many questions," Marian said, gently, stepping forward to guide him, carefully, toward the door. "You've had a long, tiring investigation, Witchfinder. Perhaps it's time you allowed yourself to rest, and to wonder, someday, whether all of it was worth quite so much of your patience."

---

**[PLATE 5 — Crane and his men leaving, confused and diminished, the immediate danger passed]**

Crane left without further protest, his two silent guards supporting him more than escorting him, the whole party moving with the particular unsteady confusion of men who understood, dimly, that something significant had happened without being able to properly name what.

It was a long while after they'd finally vanished from sight that anyone remaining felt able to move again.

"Is he—" Robin started, unable to quite finish the question.

"He'll recover, physically," Thistlewood said, still visibly unsteady himself, leaning more heavily on his staff than Robin had ever seen him need to. "His certainty won't. Not fully. Whatever he remembers of today, he'll never again be able to properly trust it, and a man who can't trust his own evidence makes for a poor witness against anyone, in any court that matters."

"The secret holds," Marian said, quiet, though there was no triumph in it, only the particular exhausted relief of a woman who had spent five chapters bracing for considerably worse than this.

---

"For now," Thistlewood said. "Perhaps for good. I don't know yet what today has actually cost us, in the longer accounting. I only know it cost me considerably more than I expected, and that I'd rather not learn what a second working of that particular magic might demand of me, should the need for it ever arise again."

He looked, finally, properly, at Robin — the weight of everything unsaid between them for so long finally, visibly settling into the space where it could no longer be avoided.

"I imagine you have questions," he said, quiet. "A great many of them, likely, and I owe you honest answers to at least some of them, eventually. But I find I haven't the strength left tonight to give them the care they deserve. Might they wait, just a little longer, for a day when I can answer them properly rather than simply exhausted?"

---

**[PLATE 6 — closing: Robin processing the truth, the group's changed dynamic, real weight but genuine hope]**

Robin considered this for a long moment, turning over a year's worth of improbable luck, of quiet impossible interventions, of a truth he had spent months circling without ever quite letting himself land on directly.

"They can wait," he said, finally. "Today's given me rather more than I know how to properly hold already. I don't need the rest of it settled by tonight." He looked between Thistlewood and Pip, something warm and unguarded working behind his own careful composure. "I'm simply glad to finally know. Whatever comes with knowing. I'd rather carry the truth than keep circling something I could feel but never quite name."

"That's rather more grace than I had any right to expect from you," Thistlewood said, quiet.

"You've earned considerably more grace than you seem to think you have," Robin said, "given everything you've apparently been doing for me, however much of it I still don't understand."

That night, once the immediate danger had properly settled and Thistlewood had finally been persuaded to rest, the camp gathered in a quiet considerably different from any they'd shared before — not merely relief at survival, but something deeper, a genuine shift in the shape of what they all now understood themselves to be.

"Everything's different now," Will observed, watching Robin and Pip talk quietly near the fire, easy in a way that hadn't existed between them that morning. "I don't quite know what to do with that."

"Nothing's different that actually mattered," Marian said, watching the same scene with something close to real peace behind her usual careful composure. "We were always exactly who we've been all along. Robin simply knows the whole shape of it now, same as I have for a while." She looked toward Thistlewood's sleeping form, toward Pip's quiet laughter, toward a year's worth of impossible luck finally, properly understood. "That's not an ending, Will. That's simply honesty, arriving later than it should have, but arriving all the same."

Somewhere beyond the firelight, a Witchfinder walked home with a memory he could no longer trust, and somewhere considerably closer, a family — for that was, at last, the only honest word for what they'd become — settled into a truth that had cost them dearly, and given them, in return, something none of them would have traded back for anything.
